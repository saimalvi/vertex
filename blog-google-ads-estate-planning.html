Google Ads for Estate Planning Attorneys: Turn Will and Trust Searches Into Signed Clients

VL
Vertex Law MarketingPublished 7 November 2026 · 10 min read

An estate planning client rarely searches the way a personal injury client does. There is no accident, no arrest, no deadline measured in hours. There is usually a moment instead — a baby is born, a parent is diagnosed, a house is bought, a will is found to be twenty years out of date — and the search that follows is quiet, careful, and easy to lose.

That changes what Google Ads has to do for an estate planning firm. You are not buying urgency. You are buying the right to be the firm a careful person calls first — and the firm they still remember a month later, when they are finally ready to book.

Estate planning clicks also cost far less than the clicks personal injury firms fight over, which makes this one of the most forgiving places in legal marketing to run paid search — if the campaign is built around how these clients actually decide. Here is how.

1. Estate planning searches are different — respect the decision

The first mistake is to run an estate planning campaign the way you would run a criminal defense or personal injury campaign. Those clients decide in hours. Estate planning clients decide in weeks, sometimes months. They will compare firms, read a page, ask a spouse, and come back later — or not at all.

That slow decision shapes everything:

  • The click is cheap; the decision is slow. Winning means being visible at the trigger moment and still credible at the decision moment, weeks later. A campaign judged after seven days will look like a failure even when it is working.
  • Trust beats cleverness. No fear tactics, no countdown timers, no promised outcomes. A calm, local, specific firm wins this work — a loud one repels exactly the clients you want.
  • The trigger is the targeting. New parents, new homeowners, adult children of aging parents, business owners, recent retirees — each group searches differently and worries about something different. Your ad copy and landing pages should name the trigger, not just the practice area.
  • The searcher is often not the client. Adult children frequently research on a phone for a parent. Your pages need to speak to both of them: the person who will sign, and the person doing the searching.

Build the campaign around that reality and estate planning becomes one of the steadiest sources of signed matters a firm can buy. Ignore it, and you will pay for clicks from people who were never going to hire anyone this week — and conclude, wrongly, that Google Ads does not work for estate planning.

2. Start with the searches that sign clients

Not every estate planning search is worth buying. The work is to separate the searches that become consultations from the searches that become free advice.

The searches that sign clients:

  • “Estate planning attorney near me” and “wills and trusts lawyer [your city]”
  • “Revocable living trust attorney” and “living trust lawyer [your city]”
  • “Probate lawyer [your city]” and “probate attorney near me”
  • “Power of attorney lawyer” and “guardianship attorney [your city]”

The searches that compare — worth bidding on, carefully:

  • “How much does a will cost” and “estate planning attorney cost”
  • “Trust vs will” and “do I need a trust”
  • “How much does probate cost” and “how long does probate take”

These searchers are earlier in the decision, but they are deciding — and a clear, honest page that answers the question earns the consultation more often than a sales page does.

The searches to keep out: “free will template”, “how to write a will”, “will forms online”, and anything with “DIY” in it. These searchers want a document, not a lawyer, and they will click your ad, read nothing, and leave. Build one ad group per matter — wills, trusts, probate, powers of attorney, guardianship — so the ad and the landing page match the search exactly, and add a negative keyword list from day one: free, template, form, DIY, sample, jobs, salary, software, online will maker, how to become.

On match types, start with phrase and exact match for the searches that sign clients. Broad match has its place, but only with a tight negative list and close watching — left alone, it will spend your budget on the free-template searches you meant to exclude. 3. Write ads for the person, not the practice area

The searcher types a problem, not a practice area. “Wills and trusts attorney” says what you are. “Protect your family with a will that actually holds up” says what they get. The second ad wins the click from the person who is ready — and it filters out the person who is not.

For responsive search ads, mix four kinds of headline:

  • The matter, plainly stated: “Estate Planning Attorney in [Your City]” and “Wills & Trusts Lawyer Near You”
  • The trigger: “New Baby? Get Your Will in Place” and “Protect the Home You Just Bought”
  • The reassurance: “Clear Flat Fees, Explained Up Front” — only if your fees genuinely work that way — and “Free Initial Consultation”, only if you offer one
  • The local signal: “Serving [Your City] Families” and “Local Estate Planning Attorneys”

Two rules hold for every ad you write. First, never promise an outcome: “we will protect your assets — guaranteed” is the kind of claim that draws bar complaints and repels careful clients. Say what you will do — review the situation, explain the options honestly, prepare the documents carefully — and let the consultation carry the rest. Second, make calling easy: add call assets and location assets to every campaign, because many estate planning callers, especially older ones, would rather call than type, and a phone number in the ad is often the difference between a click and a conversation.

A general, honest statement about what happens without a plan — that without a will, the law decides who inherits — is fair game in ad copy, because it is a plain fact about how intestacy works. A threat is not. The line between the two is the line between a firm that educates and a firm that frightens, and your clients can tell the difference.

4. Send every click to a page built for that search

The homepage is where estate planning budgets go to die. A person who searched for a revocable living trust and lands on a general homepage has to find the trusts content themselves — and most visitors will not. They will leave, and you will have paid for the privilege.

Build one landing page per matter, and send each ad group to its own page:

  • A wills page for wills searches — what a will does, what it costs in general terms, what to bring to the first meeting, and what the consultation covers
  • A trusts page for trusts searches — the difference between a will and a trust in plain language, when a trust is worth considering, and the steps involved
  • A probate page for probate searches — how the process usually works, what affects timing, and what the family needs to do first

Every one of those pages needs the same four things: a headline that repeats the search, so the visitor knows they are in the right place; a short form — name, phone or email, and one line about the situation, nothing else at this stage; a click-to-call button that stays visible on a phone; and a clear next step, stated plainly. If you offer a free initial consultation, say so. If the consultation has a fee, say what it includes. Uncertainty about cost is one of the biggest reasons people do not call.

Our guide to website design that converts covers the conversion structure in detail; for paid search, the discipline is even stricter, because every visitor arrived at a price. A page that ranks first but cannot convert a visitor has wasted the ranking — and a paid click that lands on the wrong page has wasted the money twice over.

5. What estate planning clicks cost — and how to set a budget

Here is the good news, stated carefully: estate planning keywords usually cost a fraction of what personal injury firms pay per click. The competition is thinner, the intent is calmer, and fewer firms bid aggressively. That is an advantage — not a reason to be careless, because cheap clicks still waste money if they go to the wrong page or the wrong search.

Budget by matter, not by account. Pick one matter first — wills or trusts, whichever your firm handles most — in one city or county, with one landing page, and judge it properly. As an illustration, a firm testing wills and trusts in a mid-size city might start with a modest monthly test budget and judge it after six to eight weeks, which is long enough for the slow decision cycle to play out. The figure matters less than the discipline: one matter, one geography, one landing page, and a decision rule agreed in advance.

Judge the test on cost per consultation and cost per signed matter, not on clicks. A campaign that produces ten cheap clicks and no consultations is worse than one that produces three expensive clicks and one signed trust. When the cost per signed matter is acceptable and your intake can handle more volume, raise the budget — and add the next matter, probate and then powers of attorney, rather than widening the geography first. Depth in one market beats thin coverage of three.

One warning: do not judge an estate planning campaign the way you would judge a DUI campaign. A DUI searcher calls tonight. An estate planning searcher may call in six weeks, after a conversation with their spouse and a second look at your reviews. If you switch the campaign off after ten days of quiet, you will never learn whether it worked — and you will hand the searches back to the firm that waited. 6. Local targeting, Local Services Ads, and the trust layer

Estate planning is local work, and your targeting should behave that way. Target the city and county you actually serve, exclude the areas you do not, and check your location settings so that “near me” searches resolve to people who are actually near you. A campaign that quietly spends in three counties you never visit is not a targeting strategy — it is a leak.

Where they are available, Local Services Ads are worth testing. For firms that qualify, they place the firm above the search ads with a Google Screened badge — a trust signal that matters more in estate planning than in almost any other practice area, because the client is choosing someone to handle their family’s future. The badge is not a substitute for a good campaign, but it is a strong complement to one.

Reviews feed both channels. Ask for a review at the natural moment — after the documents are signed, not during a family crisis — and never offer anything in exchange for one. And never publish a client’s story, even anonymised, without proper consent: in this practice area, a family’s circumstances are private by definition, and a firm that treats them casually will be noticed.

Finally, set up call tracking before you spend anything serious: one number per campaign, so you know which matter and which ad produced the call. Without it, you are guessing which half of the budget works — and guessing is what the budget was supposed to replace. Our guide to ranking in the Google Map Pack covers the local visibility side; paid search and local visibility should be pulling in the same direction, toward the same pages and the same phone number.

7. The follow-up is where estate planning cases are won

Most estate planning leads will not book on the first visit. That is normal — and it is where most firms lose the case they already paid for. The follow-up has two speeds, and you need both.

First, the fast one. A person who has worked up the courage to contact a lawyer and hears nothing for two days will usually contact another firm — or decide to put the whole thing off. Neither outcome helps anyone. Aim to respond within the hour during business hours, and say plainly when you will reply if the inquiry arrives outside them.

Then, the patient one. A short email sequence for people who inquired but have not booked yet: what happens in a consultation, what documents to gather, the difference between a will and a trust in plain language, and an invitation to a seminar or webinar if you run one. Nothing pushy, nothing frightening — just the information a careful person needs to feel ready. Track every inquiry from first click to signed matter in your intake system, so you know which matter, which message, and which page earns its keep.

And make the consultation itself easy to say yes to. Say what it covers, what it costs, and how long it takes. Offer an evening call or a video call, and make it clear a spouse is welcome. For many clients, the consultation is a joint decision, and a firm that makes room for that wins the booking.

8. Measure signed matters, not clicks

Estate planning marketing is easy to misjudge, because traffic is a poor proxy for cases. A blog post about a celebrity will dispute may draw visitors who will never hire you. A trusts page with modest traffic that books consultations every week is worth far more.

Track, at minimum:

  • Calls and form submissions by page and by matter, so you know which pages earn their keep
  • Consultations booked — and consultations that become signed matters — by source and by matter
  • Cost per consultation and cost per signed matter, by campaign
  • Time from first inquiry to signing, so you know how long to judge a campaign before changing it

Review those numbers monthly, not daily. Search visibility moves slowly, the decision cycle is measured in weeks, and a weekly panic about one ranking position leads to constant, unhelpful changes. A monthly review of signed matters per campaign tells you what to expand, what to rewrite, and what to stop funding.

9. The mistakes that burn an estate planning budget

Most wasted estate planning spend comes from a short list of avoidable errors:

  • Bidding on free and DIY terms without a negative list — the fastest way to buy clicks from people who will never hire a lawyer.
  • Sending paid clicks to the homepage instead of a page built for the search.
  • Broad match with no negatives, left to spend unsupervised.
  • Ads that promise outcomes — “guaranteed asset protection” and similar claims risk bar complaints and repel the careful clients you want most.
  • No call tracking, so the firm cannot tell which ads produce consultations and which only produce clicks.
  • Ignoring mobile. Many searchers are adult children researching on a phone for a parent — if the page is hard to read or the form is hard to complete on a phone, they leave.
  • Judging the campaign after a week, when the decision cycle is measured in weeks.
  • Switching the campaign off the moment it works. Pausing resets the learning, hands the searches back to competitors, and means paying to rebuild the same position later.

Where to start this month

You do not need to rebuild everything at once. For most small-to-midsize estate planning firms, this order returns the most signed matters for the least spend:

  • This week: pick one matter — wills or trusts — build or fix its landing page, shorten the form to the essentials, add a visible click-to-call button, and set up call tracking
  • This month: launch a small, tightly targeted campaign for that matter in your city, with a negative list from day one and a clear consultation offer
  • This quarter: add the next matter, test Local Services Ads if they are available to you, systematise review requests, and review cost per signed matter before spending more

Estate planning clients are not looking for the loudest firm in their city. They are looking for the firm that understands what prompted the search, explains the next step clearly, answers when it matters, and is still there — calm, local, and credible — when they are ready to decide. Be that firm in your ads, and your marketing will stop being a clicks exercise and start being a case pipeline.

Key Takeaways

  • Estate planning searches are triggered by life events, not emergencies — the click is cheap and the decision is slow.
  • Bid on the searches that sign clients — wills, trusts, probate, powers of attorney — and keep free and DIY terms out with a negative list.
  • Write ads for the person and the trigger, not the practice area, and keep every claim honest and local.
  • One landing page per matter, a short form, and a visible phone number — never send paid clicks to the homepage.
  • Judge the campaign on cost per consultation and cost per signed matter, not on clicks.
  • Follow up fast and nurture patiently: most estate planning clients need weeks, not hours, to decide.
  • Start with one matter, one city, and a modest test budget — then expand what proves itself.
