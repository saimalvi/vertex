Google Ads for Immigration Law Firms: Turn Visa, Green Card, and Asylum Searches Into Signed Clients

VL
Vertex Law MarketingPublished 23 November 2026 · 10 min read

An immigration client rarely searches the way other legal clients search. They are not choosing between firms on a quiet afternoon. They are often searching in a second language, on a phone, with a deadline attached — a visa expiring, a court date approaching, a family member waiting for an answer. They type “immigration lawyer near me” or “green card lawyer cost” and they call the first firm that looks able to help, often within minutes.

That urgency is exactly why Google Ads works so well for immigration firms — and exactly why it can waste money faster than most firms expect. Immigration searches cover an enormous range of needs, languages, and budgets, and a campaign that treats them all the same will pay for clicks from people who were never going to become clients. The firms that win are the ones whose campaigns are built for the way an immigration client actually searches, in the language they search in, and at the moment they are ready to act.

Here is how immigration firms use Google Ads to turn visa, green card, and asylum searches into signed clients — and how to stop paying for clicks that were never going to become cases.

1. Immigration is many practices in one — build campaigns that way

The first mistake most immigration firms make in Google Ads is running one campaign called “immigration”. That single word covers family visas, employment visas, asylum, deportation defense, citizenship, DACA, and business immigration — each with different searchers, different urgency, different budgets, and different value to the firm. A campaign that lumps them together cannot bid correctly on any of them.

Split your campaigns by practice area first, and let each one have its own budget, its own ads, and its own landing pages. A family-based green card campaign should never share a budget with a deportation defense campaign: the first searcher may take weeks to decide, while the second may need a lawyer this week. If they share one budget, the cheaper, slower clicks will quietly consume the money that should have bought the urgent ones.

Start with the practice areas that produce your best cases, and build outward from there:

  • Family immigration: green cards through marriage or family, fiancé visas, adjustment of status, consular processing. High volume, long decision cycles, and searchers who compare several firms before calling.
  • Employment immigration: H-1B, L-1, O-1, PERM, and employer-sponsored green cards. Searchers are often employers as well as employees, and they value speed and a clear process.
  • Asylum and humanitarian: asylum applications, withholding of removal, VAWA, U visas, T visas. Deeply personal searches, often in another language, where trust matters more than anything else on the page.
  • Deportation defense: removal proceedings, immigration court, bond hearings, appeals. The most urgent searches in immigration law — and the ones where answering the phone first wins the case.
  • Citizenship: naturalization, citizenship through parents, denaturalization defense. Lower urgency, higher volume, and a good entry point for firms building an immigration practice.

2. Bid on the searches that can become clients — in the right order

Not every immigration search is worth the same money. The highest-intent searches name the need and the lawyer in the same breath: “immigration lawyer near me”, “green card attorney Chicago”, “asylum lawyer”, “deportation lawyer”. Those are the searches to win first, and to bid most aggressively on, because the searcher has already decided they need a lawyer and is choosing which one to call.

Below those, a second tier of searches is still valuable but should be bid on separately, at lower bids, with different ads: “how long does a green card take”, “how much does an immigration lawyer cost”, “can I apply for asylum after one year”. These searchers are earlier in their decision. They may become clients — often after a consultation that answers the question honestly — but they convert at a lower rate, and they should not share a budget with the searches that are ready to call now.

Keep the two tiers in separate campaigns with separate budgets. And remember that paid search does not work alone: our guide to ranking in the Google Map Pack covers the local visibility system, and the firms that win both channels own the first screen for immigration searches in their city.

  • Highest intent: practice area + lawyer or attorney + city, and “near me” versions of the same. Bid here first.
  • High intent: specific situations — “marriage green card lawyer”, “H-1B visa attorney”, “immigration court lawyer”, “citizenship lawyer near me”. Separate ad groups, matched landing pages.
  • Research intent: processing times, fees, eligibility questions, “do I need a lawyer for a green card”. Lower bids, helpful landing pages, and a clear path to a consultation.
  • Not worth bidding on: free legal help, legal aid, pro bono, USCIS forms and instructions, government fee schedules, and do-it-yourself filing guides. These belong on your negative keyword list, not in your campaigns.

3. Advertise in the languages your clients actually search in

This is the single biggest difference between immigration Google Ads and every other practice area — and the one most firms miss entirely. A large share of immigration clients search in Spanish, and many others search in Mandarin, Hindi, Arabic, Portuguese, Vietnamese, or other languages. A firm that only advertises in English is invisible to a large part of its own market.

Build separate campaigns for each language you can genuinely serve, with ads and landing pages written in that language — not machine-translated, and not an English page with a translated headline. A Spanish-speaking searcher who clicks a Spanish ad and lands on an English page will leave, and you will have paid for that click.

  • Start with Spanish. For most US immigration firms, Spanish-language searches are the largest non-English segment, and competition for them is often lighter than for the equivalent English keywords — which means the clicks can cost less while converting just as well.
  • Match the language to the staff. Only advertise in a language your firm can actually serve: the intake call, the consultation, and the casework all need to happen in that language. An ad in a language nobody at the firm speaks is a promise the firm cannot keep.
  • Use the right keywords in the right language. Spanish searchers do not always translate the English keyword literally. “Abogado de inmigración” is the obvious one, but searches like “abogado para la residencia”, “abogado de asilo”, and “abogado de deportación cerca de mí” are the ones that become clients.
  • Keep language campaigns separate. Different languages, different budgets, different landing pages, and different performance. Mixing them into one campaign hides which language is actually producing clients.
  • Note the language in the ad. A line like “Se habla español” in an English ad, or a fully Spanish ad for Spanish searches, tells the searcher immediately that they will be understood — and that is often the deciding factor.

Language is not a translation task. It is a targeting decision, and it is one of the few places in Google Ads where a smaller firm can outmaneuver a larger one simply by speaking to the searcher in their own words.

4. Match the ad to the immigration need, not to the firm

Most immigration ads read the same: the firm name, “free consultation”, “se habla español”, and a phone number. A searcher facing a deadline skims past that, because it does not tell them whether this firm handles their kind of case.

Build one ad group per immigration need — family green cards, employment visas, asylum, deportation defense, citizenship — and write the ads for that need alone. A green card ad should say green card, name the free consultation, and say what the first call covers. An asylum ad should say asylum, because a searcher seeking asylum is looking for a firm that understands their situation specifically.

  • Use the searcher’s words in the headline. If the search was “green card lawyer”, the headline should say green card lawyer, not “immigration help available”.
  • Say what happens when they call. A free, confidential consultation, a clear explanation of the process and the fees, and a real person answering — not a form and a wait.
  • Use call assets and location assets. On mobile, most immigration clicks should become phone calls, not website visits. Make the phone number one tap away, and show the office location so the searcher knows the firm is local.
  • Test call-only ads for the highest-intent searches. When someone searches “immigration lawyer near me” at 8 p.m. from a phone, the fastest path to a signed client is a call, not a landing page. Call-only ads remove the step in between.
  • Keep the tone steady and reassuring. Immigration clients are often anxious, and some are afraid. Fear-based copy and promises about outcomes repel the careful clients you want most — and advertising rules in many states limit what a firm can promise about results. Say what you will do: listen, review the facts, explain the options honestly, and pursue the best result the law allows.

The same rule applies to the page the ad leads to. An ad about asylum that lands on a general immigration homepage is a broken promise, and a searcher under pressure will not wait to work out where the asylum information lives.

5. Build landing pages that convert a worried visitor in seconds

An immigration click is bought from a visitor who is often worried, sometimes in a hurry, and frequently reading in a second language. The landing page has to earn the click immediately, because the visitor is comparing you against two other firms in other tabs.

The first screen should answer three things before anything else: do you handle my kind of case, what does it cost to talk to you, and how fast will someone respond. A phone number that is one tap away, a short form — name, phone, and one line about the situation — and a plain statement that the consultation is free or low-cost and confidential. Everything else — attorney biographies, office photos, practice descriptions — belongs further down, after the visitor has decided the firm might be able to help.

  • One page per immigration need. The green card page talks about green cards: the process, the timeline, the fees, and what the first consultation covers. The asylum page does the same for asylum. A page that genuinely answers the searcher’s question is the page that converts — and it is also the page Google rewards with a better quality score, which lowers what you pay per click.
  • Offer the page in the searcher’s language. A Spanish ad must land on a Spanish page. A page that switches language halfway through, or that offers only a translated headline above English text, loses the visitor it just paid to reach.
  • Load fast on a phone. A slow page on a mobile connection loses the click you just paid for. Keep images light, remove anything that delays the phone number and the form, and test the page on a real phone, not only on a desktop.
  • Show real people. Real photos of the attorneys and staff, a real office address, and a real phone number. Stock photos of handshakes tell a worried visitor nothing — and in this practice area, anonymity reads as a warning sign.
  • Do not ask for what you do not need yet. Case numbers, immigration documents, and detailed histories come after the first conversation, not before it. A form that asks for them up front signals a firm that does not understand the visitor’s situation.
  • Confirm what happens next. After the form is submitted, say exactly what happens: who will call, when, in what language, and what they will ask. A confirmation page that says nothing is a missed chance to reassure someone who is already anxious.

6. Cut the wasted spend with negative keywords

In immigration, the difference between a profitable campaign and a money pit is often the negative keyword list. Because the searches are so broad — from visa forms to government fees to free legal help — an unfocused campaign will pay for a great many clicks that could never become clients.

Start with the obvious exclusions and review your search terms report every week to find more:

  • Jobs and education: jobs, hiring, salary, law school, paralegal, internship, “how to become an immigration lawyer”
  • Free and DIY: free legal help, pro bono, legal aid, “file my own green card”, USCIS forms, “how to apply for a visa myself”, form downloads and instructions
  • Government information: USCIS processing times, visa bulletin, passport applications, embassy appointments, government fees — unless you are deliberately running an informational campaign, which is a separate decision with its own budget
  • Wrong places: cities and states you do not serve, and countries you do not handle cases from, unless the searcher is in your market
  • Wrong case types: if you do not handle employment visas, exclude them; if you do not take deportation defense cases, exclude those terms rather than paying for their clicks
  • Wrong languages you cannot serve: if your firm cannot serve a language, exclude its keywords rather than paying for calls you cannot take

The search terms report is where this work happens. Every week, look at what people actually typed before they clicked your ad, and add every term that could not have become a client to the negative list. Over a month, this single habit can cut a meaningful share of wasted spend — and the savings go straight into the searches that do become clients.

7. Win the call: intake speed and language are the real conversion rate

Immigration clients call the firm that answers — in their language. A searcher facing a deadline will often call two or three firms in a row; the one that picks up first, and speaks their language, usually gets the case. That makes intake — not the ad copy, not the bid — the single biggest lever on your cost per signed client.

  • Answer in minutes, in the caller’s language. A Spanish-speaking caller who reaches an English-only intake line will hang up and call the next firm. Staff the languages you advertise in, or use a trained bilingual answering service for the hours you cannot cover.
  • Keep the first call short and human. The caller needs to hear that the firm handles their kind of case, what the consultation costs, and what the next step is. A script that asks for documents before it offers reassurance loses callers.
  • Follow up once, well. A caller who does not answer the first callback is not a lost client. One prompt, polite follow-up — a call and a short message in the caller’s language — recovers cases a single attempt would lose. More than that becomes pressure.
  • Track the call, not just the click. Call tracking numbers per campaign and per language, and a note in your intake system of which campaign and keyword produced each call, turn Google Ads from a traffic bill into a client pipeline you can judge.

This is also where many firms discover their ads were working all along and their intake was not. Before raising the budget, check how many calls went unanswered, how many callers could not be served in their language, how long callbacks took, and how many consultations never got booked. Fixing intake is usually cheaper than buying more clicks.

8. Target the geography and the hours where clients search

Immigration is a local practice for most firms, and your campaigns should be built that way. A firm in one city should not be paying for clicks in another state, and a firm that serves a large immigrant community should know which neighborhoods and suburbs produce its clients.

  • Target where you actually practise. Set location targeting to the cities and counties you serve, and use “people in your targeted locations” rather than “people searching about your locations”, so a searcher in another state looking up your city does not cost you a click.
  • Think in communities, not just cities. Immigration clients often cluster by neighborhood and community. When the data shows one area signs clients at a lower cost than another, shift budget toward it. Over a quarter, that reallocation alone can move your cost per signed client noticeably.
  • Schedule ads for when people search — and when you can answer. Immigration searches spike in the evening and on weekends, when people are free to research and call. But if no one can answer the phone at 10 p.m., paying for those clicks wastes them. Either staff the hours or schedule the ads around the hours you can genuinely serve, and use call scheduling so the phone number only shows when someone is there to pick up.
  • Separate mobile from desktop. Most immigration searches happen on a phone, and mobile clicks convert differently — usually into calls, at a different rate and cost. Keep mobile and desktop performance visible separately so you are not judging a call-heavy mobile campaign by desktop form-fill standards.

9. Budget by signed client, not by click

The question most immigration firms ask first — how much should we spend on Google Ads — is the wrong question until you know what a signed client is worth to the firm and what you are willing to pay to sign one. Our guide to how much a law firm should spend on Google Ads gives the budgeting framework in full; for immigration, three rules matter most.

  • Work back from the client, not forward from the click. Decide what a signed client is worth and what you can afford to pay to sign one, then judge the campaign by cost per signed client. A campaign with expensive clicks and a low cost per signed client is a good campaign; a campaign with cheap clicks and no signed clients is a waste of money.
  • Give a new campaign enough budget to learn. A budget so small that it buys a handful of clicks a day will never produce enough data to judge, and pausing it after a week tells you nothing. Fund it properly for a full month, with the structure in place, before deciding whether it works.
  • Reallocate, do not just increase. When one practice area signs clients at a lower cost than another, move budget toward it. When one language produces consultations that never sign, check the intake before blaming the ads — and when one keyword produces calls that never sign, pause it and give the money to the keywords that do. Most immigration campaigns improve more from reallocation than from a bigger total budget.

Judge the spend monthly, not daily. Click costs move with competition, and a single expensive day means nothing. A month of cost per consultation and cost per signed client, by campaign, by practice area, and by language, tells you what to expand, what to fix, and what to stop.

10. Measure signed clients, not clicks

Google Ads reports clicks, impressions, and click-through rates by default. None of those is a client. For an immigration firm, the numbers that matter are consultations booked, consultations that become signed clients, and cost per signed client — by campaign, by practice area, by language, and by keyword where the data allows.

  • Connect the ad to the signed client. Use call tracking, form tracking, and your intake or case management system together, so a signed client can be traced back to the campaign that produced it. Without that link, you are guessing which half of the budget works.
  • Import offline conversions. A click becomes a consultation, and a consultation becomes a signed client, days or weeks later. Feeding signed clients back into Google Ads as offline conversions lets the system bid toward the searches that actually produce clients, not just the ones that produce clicks.
  • Review search terms weekly, performance monthly. The search terms report catches wasted spend early; the monthly review of cost per signed client is where the real decisions happen — which practice areas to grow, which languages to expand, which keywords to pause, and where the next dollar goes.
  • Keep the rest of the funnel honest. Ads bring the call, but the website, the intake, and the follow-up sign the client. Where to start this month

You do not need to rebuild everything at once. For most small-to-midsize immigration firms, this order returns the most signed clients for the least spend:

  • This week: split your highest-intent keywords into their own campaign by practice area, add the obvious negative keywords, and check how many calls went unanswered last week — and in which languages
  • This month: launch one non-English campaign in the language your firm serves best, with ads and landing pages written in that language; rebuild your top three landing pages — green cards, asylum, and citizenship — with one-tap calling, a short form, and a clear statement of what happens next; start reviewing the search terms report weekly
  • This quarter: connect signed clients back to campaigns with call tracking and offline conversion imports, then reallocate budget toward the practice areas and languages with the lowest cost per signed client

Immigration clients are not looking for the loudest firm in their city. They are looking for the firm that answers in their language, understands their kind of case, explains what it costs and what happens next, and follows up when it matters. Be that firm in the ad, on the page, and on the phone, and Google Ads stops being the most uncertain line in your marketing budget and starts being the most reliable source of signed clients you have.

Key Takeaways

  • Immigration is many practices in one: split campaigns by practice area — family, employment, asylum, deportation defense, citizenship — each with its own budget, ads, and landing pages.
  • Bid hardest on practice area + lawyer + city searches, lower on research questions, and never let the two share one budget.
  • Advertise in the languages your clients search in, with ads and landing pages written in that language — and only in languages your firm can genuinely serve.
  • One ad group per immigration need, with ads and landing pages written for that need alone; an asylum ad should never land on a general homepage.
  • Negative keywords and a weekly search terms review are the fastest way to cut wasted spend in immigration campaigns.
  • Intake speed and language are the real conversion rate: the firm that answers first, in the caller’s language, usually signs the client.
  • Target the communities you serve, schedule ads around the hours you can answer, and judge mobile and desktop performance separately.
  • Budget by cost per signed client, not cost per click — and feed signed clients back into Google Ads as offline conversions so the system bids toward real clients.
