Google Ads for Criminal Defense Attorneys: Win the Urgent Searches That Become Clients

VL
Vertex Law MarketingPublished 11 November 2026 · 10 min read

A criminal defense client does not shop around. They search fast, usually on a phone, often within hours of an arrest — sometimes from a police station parking lot, sometimes at 2 a.m. after a call from a family member. They are not comparing law firms the way someone compares estate planners. They are looking for one firm that answers, sounds capable, and can help right now.

That is what makes Google Ads both the best and the hardest channel for a criminal defense firm. It puts your firm at the top of the page at the exact moment someone needs a lawyer — and it charges you for every click, including the ones from people who were never going to hire you. The firms that make it work are not the ones with the biggest budgets. They are the ones that buy only the searches that mean “I need a lawyer now,” send those clicks to a page built for a frightened person, and answer the phone when it rings.

Here is how that kind of campaign is built.

1. Criminal defense is Google Ads’ hardest — and best — test

Most legal advertising is slow. A person thinking about a will might search for weeks before calling anyone. A person who has just been arrested does not have weeks. The search happens fast, the decision happens faster, and the first firm that answers competently often gets the case.

That urgency is the opportunity. It is also the reason criminal defense keywords are among the most expensive clicks in legal advertising. Every firm in your city wants the same searches, and Google charges accordingly. A campaign that treats criminal defense like any other practice area — broad keywords, one generic ad, a homepage as the landing page — will spend its budget on curiosity clicks and leave the real cases to the firm down the street.

Three facts shape everything that follows:

  • The searcher is usually not the defendant. Often it is a spouse, a parent, or a friend searching on someone else’s behalf. The ad and the landing page have to speak to them too: “someone you love was just arrested” is a different message from “you were just arrested.”
  • Most of it happens on a phone, after hours. Weeknights and weekends are peak time. If your ads only run during office hours, or your site is hard to use on a phone, you are invisible exactly when the cases are searching.
  • Trust has to be established in seconds. The searcher cannot evaluate your legal skill from an ad. They judge you on whether you answer, whether you sound like you have handled this charge before, and whether the next step is obvious. Everything in the campaign should serve that judgment.

2. Start with the searches that mean “I need a lawyer now”

Keyword selection is where most criminal defense campaigns are won or lost. The goal is not traffic; it is intent. A click from someone who types “criminal defense lawyer near me” is worth far more than ten clicks from someone who types “what happens if you get arrested.”

Sort your keywords into three tiers, and spend accordingly:

  • Emergency intent — spend most here. Searches like “criminal defense lawyer near me,” “DUI lawyer [city],” “drug possession attorney,” “assault charges lawyer,” and “domestic violence attorney near me.” These people are looking to hire. They are also the most expensive clicks, which is exactly why they deserve the budget.
  • Charge-specific questions — spend some here. Searches like “first DUI penalties [state],” “can domestic violence charges be dropped,” or “how long does a drug charge stay on your record.” These searchers are earlier in the process, but they have a real problem and a real reason to call. Send them to a page that answers the question and offers a consultation, not to a generic contact page.
  • Research and definitions — mostly avoid. Searches like “what is a misdemeanor,” “difference between felony and misdemeanor,” or “criminal law definition.” These are students, journalists, and the curious. They rarely become clients, and at criminal defense click prices, they are an expensive way to educate the internet.

Negative keywords matter as much as the keywords you buy. Add terms like “free,” “pro bono,” “jobs,” “salary,” “law school,” “how to become,” and “public defender” as negatives, and review your search terms report every week for the first month. A campaign that nobody watches will quietly spend a large share of its budget on people who were never going to hire a private attorney. 3. Build one campaign per charge, not one campaign for the firm

The most common structural mistake in criminal defense advertising is a single campaign with one ad group called “criminal defense” and a keyword list that mixes DUI, drug charges, assault, and theft. Google cannot match a DUI search to a generic ad about criminal defense as well as it can match it to an ad about DUI defense — and the searcher can tell the difference too.

Structure the account the way clients actually search:

  • One campaign per charge type. DUI, drug possession, assault, domestic violence, theft, and white collar — each gets its own campaign, its own budget, and its own landing page. That way a DUI click never lands on a page about assault, and you can see which charge produces signed cases instead of guessing.
  • One ad group per search theme inside each campaign. Inside the DUI campaign, separate “DUI lawyer” searches from “first DUI penalties” searches. The ad and the landing page should match the exact question the person typed.
  • Separate budgets by case value and volume. Some charges bring more searches and more signed cases than others, and some cases are worth far more to the firm. Give the budget to the campaigns that produce clients, not the ones that produce clicks. Review this monthly, not once a year.
  • Keep geography tight. Criminal defense is a local decision made under pressure. A radius around your office and the courthouses you practice in will outperform a whole-state campaign, because the searcher wants a lawyer who knows their court, not a firm three hours away. Location targeting should follow where you actually take cases, not where you wish you did.

This structure takes longer to build than a single campaign. It is also the difference between a campaign you can manage and one you can only watch spend. 4. Write ads that sound like a lawyer who answers the phone

A criminal defense ad has about three seconds to do its job. The searcher is scanning a list of similar-looking firms, and the ad that wins the click is the one that feels most like help, not the one with the most superlatives.

What actually works in the ad copy:

  • Say the charge, not the practice area. “DUI Defense Attorney — Free Consultation” beats “Experienced Criminal Defense Law Firm” because it matches the search and tells the searcher they are in the right place.
  • Lead with availability. “Available 24/7,” “Call now — we answer nights and weekends,” and “Same-day consultations” are the strongest messages in this practice area, because the searcher’s real question is whether anyone will pick up.
  • Name a real credential, once. Former prosecutor, a specific number of years focused on criminal defense, or a genuine local distinction — one believable credential beats a list of adjectives. Never invent a credential, a ranking, or a result; a claim that cannot be checked will cost you the client when they do check.
  • Make the call the obvious action. Use call assets so the phone number shows in the ad, and use location assets so the searcher can see you are nearby. On a phone, the ad should be one tap from a conversation, not one tap from a form.
  • Write three or four headlines per ad and let Google test them. Mix the charge, the availability, the consultation offer, and the credential across headlines. Retire the combinations that never get clicked, and keep the ones that bring calls.

5. Send clicks to a page built for a scared person at 2 a.m.

This is where most criminal defense campaigns quietly fail. The ad does its job, the click arrives, and the visitor lands on the firm’s homepage — a page about the firm, written for everyone, with a contact form buried at the bottom. The visitor leaves in seconds, and the firm has paid for the privilege.

Every campaign needs its own landing page, and that page has one job: make it easy for a frightened person to call right now.

  • Phone number first, everywhere. A large, tappable number at the top of the page, repeated after every section. On mobile, calling should take one tap. A form is a backup for people who cannot talk right now, never the main event.
  • The charge in the headline. The visitor should see their charge in the first line of the page and know immediately they are in the right place. A DUI visitor who lands on a page headed “Criminal Defense” has to work out whether you handle DUI; a page headed “DUI Defense” has already answered.
  • Plain answers to the three questions everyone asks. What happens next, what it could cost, and what to do — and what not to do — before the first court date. Answer in plain language, with realistic ranges rather than promises. No outcome can be guaranteed, and a page that pretends otherwise is a liability, not a selling point.
  • Speed, on a phone, on a weak connection. Most criminal defense clicks come from a mobile phone, sometimes on poor signal. A page that loads in two seconds earns the call; a page that takes eight loses it. This is where website design that converts and Google Ads meet: an ad that wins a click to a page nobody can load has wasted the money twice.
  • One clear next step, repeated. Call now, or request a callback. Not a newsletter signup, not a downloadable guide, not a chat widget that asks five questions first. The visitor’s problem is urgent; the page’s response should be too.

6. Track signed clients, not clicks

Google Ads will happily report clicks, impressions, and click-through rates. None of those is a client. A campaign can look successful in the Ads dashboard and lose money in real life, because the clicks came from the wrong searches or the calls never turned into consultations.

Track what actually matters:

  • Calls, by campaign and by charge. Use call tracking so every call is tied back to the campaign, ad group, and keyword that produced it. A campaign that produces twenty calls a month is worth more than one that produces two hundred clicks.
  • Consultations, not just calls. A call that lasts eight seconds is not the same as a consultation. Track which campaigns produce real conversations, and which produce wrong numbers, solicitors, and people looking for free advice.
  • Signed cases and cost per signed case. This is the number that decides whether a campaign survives. A DUI campaign that costs more per click but signs cases is a better investment than a cheaper campaign that never signs one. Work backward from the fee a signed case is worth to decide what a click is worth paying for.
  • What happens after the call. If a campaign brings in consultations that never become clients, the problem may be the landing page setting the wrong expectation, the intake process losing people who were ready to hire, or the phones going unanswered after hours. Fix the leak before raising the budget.

Set this up before you spend, not after. Conversion tracking, call tracking, and a simple intake log take an afternoon to put in place and save months of arguing about whether the ads are working.

7. Budget and bid without burning money

Criminal defense clicks are expensive, and the temptation is to set a big daily budget and let Google spend it. That is how firms pay for clicks they did not need. A disciplined budget does the opposite: it starts narrow, learns what converts, and only then grows.

  • Start with your best charge, not all of them. Pick the one or two charges you handle most and best — for many firms that is DUI — and launch those campaigns first. Learn what a signed case costs from those campaigns, then expand to the next charge with real numbers behind you. Our DUI defense marketing guide covers the demand side of that first campaign in detail.
  • Bid on value, not on position. Being in the top ad slot for every search is expensive, and the second or third slot often produces signed cases at a far lower cost. Watch cost per consultation, not ad position.
  • Use dayparting honestly. Criminal defense searches peak in the evenings, at night, and on weekends. If your ads only run nine to five, you are paying to be visible when the fewest cases are searching. Run ads when cases search, and make sure someone — a person, not a voicemail — answers when they call.
  • Protect the budget with the search terms report. Check it weekly for the first two months. Every irrelevant search term you find is a negative keyword that stops the next wasted click. This one habit saves more money than any bidding strategy.
  • Remember SEO is the long game underneath. Google Ads buys you the top of the page today; local SEO earns you the Map Pack and organic rankings that keep bringing cases after the ad budget stops. The firms that win this practice area long term run both, and let each one make the other cheaper.

Where to start this month

You do not need to rebuild everything at once. For most small-to-midsize criminal defense firms, this order returns the most signed cases for the least spend:

  1. Pick your best charge and build one campaign for it. DUI is the usual starting point: high volume, clear intent, and a landing page you can build well.
  2. Write the keyword list in three tiers. Emergency intent first, charge-specific questions second, research terms excluded with negative keywords.
  3. Build the landing page before you launch the ads. Phone number first, the charge in the headline, plain answers, one clear next step, fast on a phone.
  4. Set up call tracking and an intake log on day one. If you cannot tie a call to a campaign, you cannot tell whether the campaign works.
  5. Review the search terms report weekly for two months. Add negatives, move budget to the campaigns that sign cases, and only then expand to the next charge.

Google Ads for criminal defense is not a trick, and it is not a race to spend the most. It is the disciplined work of buying only the searches that mean “I need a lawyer now,” answering the phone when those searches call, and measuring signed cases instead of clicks. Do that consistently, and the searches that matter — the ones typed in the hours after an arrest, when the decision is being made — will find your firm first.
