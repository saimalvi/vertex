Bankruptcy Law Firm SEO: How to Win Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 Searches in Your City

VL
Vertex Law MarketingPublished 8 November 2026 · 10 min read

A bankruptcy client rarely searches the way a personal injury client does. There is no accident and no opposing party to blame. There is usually a long, quiet slide instead — a job lost, a medical bill that never got paid, a credit card balance that outgrew the paycheck — and then a moment when the phone calls and the letters make the problem impossible to ignore.

That moment is when the search happens, and it happens in private. On a phone, late at night, with the screen tilted away from anyone else in the room. The person searching is stressed, often embarrassed, and worried about what filing will cost them — their house, their car, their reputation, their credit for years to come.

Bankruptcy SEO is the work of being the firm that person finds, trusts, and calls first. The searches are high intent, the competition in most cities is thinner than in personal injury, and the firms that win are the ones whose pages answer the frightening questions honestly instead of hiding behind legal jargon. Here is how.

1. Bankruptcy searches are different — respect the fear

The first mistake is to treat bankruptcy like any other practice area. A DUI client wants a lawyer tonight. A bankruptcy client has often been thinking about this for months and dreading it for longer. Shame is part of the decision, whether anyone admits it or not, and your website either lowers that barrier or raises it.

That reality shapes everything:

  • The search is private. Most of these visitors arrive on a phone, often outside business hours, and they will not call from a shared line or fill out a form that asks for more than they are ready to give. Your pages need to work for someone who is not ready to be identified yet.
  • The questions are frightening. “Will I lose my house?” “Will my employer find out?” “Can I keep my car?” “How long will this stay on my credit?” A page that answers these plainly earns the call. A page that hides behind “contact us to discuss your situation” does not.
  • Trust is decided in seconds. A calm, specific, local page wins. A page full of stock photos, vague promises, and pressure tactics repels exactly the clients you want.
  • The decision is fast once it starts. People delay for months, then move quickly once they decide to act — often because a foreclosure date, a garnishment, or a lawsuit has set a deadline. Be visible, be credible, and be easy to reach when that moment arrives.

Build your SEO around that person — stressed, private, and afraid of losing what they have left — and bankruptcy becomes one of the steadiest sources of signed cases a firm can earn from search. Ignore it, and you will rank for the wrong searches and wonder why the phone stays quiet.

2. Start with the searches that sign clients

Not every bankruptcy search is worth ranking for. The work is to separate the searches that become consultations from the searches that become free advice.

The searches that sign clients:

  • “Bankruptcy attorney near me” and “bankruptcy lawyer [your city]”
  • “Chapter 7 bankruptcy attorney” and “Chapter 13 bankruptcy lawyer [your city]”
  • “Stop foreclosure” and “stop wage garnishment lawyer”
  • “Bankruptcy attorney free consultation” — if you offer one, say so plainly

The searches that compare — worth ranking for, carefully:

  • “Chapter 7 vs Chapter 13” and “do I qualify for Chapter 7”
  • “How much does bankruptcy cost” and “bankruptcy attorney fees”
  • “Will I lose my house if I file bankruptcy” and “can I keep my car in bankruptcy”
  • “How long does bankruptcy stay on your credit”

These searchers are earlier in the decision, but they are deciding — and a clear, honest page that answers the question earns the consultation more often than a sales page does.

The searches to stop chasing: “how to file bankruptcy for free”, “bankruptcy forms”, “do it yourself bankruptcy”, and anything about filing without a lawyer. These searchers want a document, not a lawyer, and they will read your page and leave. Build one page per matter — Chapter 7, Chapter 13, foreclosure defense, wage garnishment — so the page matches the search exactly, and keep each page focused on the client’s question rather than on the firm’s history.

3. Win the Map Pack for “bankruptcy attorney near me”

Most bankruptcy searches are local, and most of them are decided in the Map Pack — the three firms Google shows with a map before the regular results. For a high-intent, private search like this, the Map Pack is where the call happens, often before the searcher ever scrolls to a website.

The three things that decide it are relevance, distance, and prominence, and all three are within your control:

  • Relevance: your Google Business Profile should say plainly that you handle bankruptcy, and name Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 in your services and description. A profile that lists ten practice areas equally will lose to one that is clearly a bankruptcy firm.
  • Distance: you cannot move your office, but you can be precise about the areas you serve — and you can build location pages for the cities and counties around you, so the firm shows up for searches beyond its own neighborhood.
  • Prominence: reviews, citations, and mentions. A bankruptcy firm with a steady flow of recent reviews will outrank a firm with an older, larger count that has gone quiet. Ask for a review at the natural moment — after the case is filed and the client feels relief, not during the crisis.

Keep the profile honest and current: real photos of the office, accurate hours, and the same name, address, and phone number everywhere the firm appears online. Inconsistent details are one of the quietest ranking problems a firm can have, and one of the easiest to fix.

4. Build pages that answer the questions clients are afraid to ask

Your practice-area pages are where bankruptcy SEO is won or lost. A generic page titled “Bankruptcy” that says “we handle Chapter 7 and Chapter 13” and nothing else will not rank, and will not convert the visitor it does get.

Build one page per matter, and give each page the same structure:

  • A headline that repeats the search — “Chapter 7 Bankruptcy in [Your City]” — so the visitor knows they are in the right place.
  • What it is, in plain language. Chapter 7 discharges most unsecured debts, usually in a matter of months. Chapter 13 is a court-approved repayment plan, usually over three to five years, that can help people keep a house or a car. Say it that simply, then add the detail.
  • The frightening questions, answered directly. What happens to the house, the car, the retirement account, the tax refund. What the means test actually does. What the automatic stay stops — collection calls, lawsuits, garnishment, foreclosure proceedings — and what it does not stop.
  • What the process looks like, step by step. The consultation, the paperwork, the filing, the meeting of creditors, the discharge. People fear what they cannot picture; a clear timeline lowers the fear.
  • What it costs, in general terms. You do not need to publish a price list, but a page that says nothing about cost while every other page does is a page the searcher leaves. Explain what affects the fee and what the consultation covers.
  • A short form and a visible phone number. Name, phone or email, and one line about the situation — nothing else at this stage. A person who is not ready to be identified will not complete a ten-field form.

Keep every claim honest. Never promise that a client will keep a particular asset or that a particular debt will be discharged — those answers depend on the facts, and a page that promises outcomes will draw bar complaints and repel the careful clients you want most. Say what you will do: review the situation, explain the options honestly, and file carefully. 5. Reviews, trust, and privacy — the deciding factors

For a bankruptcy client, the review section is not a nice extra. It is the evidence that other people in the same frightening position were treated well — and that the firm did what it said it would do.

A few rules hold here more than anywhere else:

  • Ask at the right moment. After the filing, when the collection calls have stopped and the client can breathe again, is the natural time. Never ask during the crisis, and never offer anything in exchange for a review.
  • Protect privacy absolutely. Never publish a client’s story, even anonymized, without proper consent. A bankruptcy is a private financial matter by definition, and a firm that treats it casually will be noticed — by clients and by regulators.
  • Answer every review. A short, professional reply to a positive review and a calm, non-defensive reply to a negative one both signal the same thing: this firm pays attention after the check clears.
  • Show the human firm. Real photos of the attorneys and the office, a short plain-language bio, and a page that says who will actually handle the case. A bankruptcy client is handing over their financial life; they want to know who is on the other side of the desk.

6. Content that ranks — and reassures

Bankruptcy content works when it answers the exact question the searcher typed, in the words they typed it. That is both an SEO rule and a trust rule: the person who searched “will I lose my house if I file bankruptcy” wants that question answered first, not three paragraphs about the firm’s history.

The content that earns rankings and consultations:

  • Question-led articles: one question per page — Chapter 7 vs Chapter 13, the means test, what happens to a car loan, how bankruptcy affects a credit score, whether a spouse has to file too. Each page should answer the question in the first paragraph and then go deeper.
  • Local pages: a page for each city and county you serve, with local detail — the courthouse, the trustee process, the local exemptions that matter — rather than the same text with a city name swapped in. Thin location pages do not rank, and they do not convince anyone.
  • FAQ content with clear structure: short questions, direct answers, and proper FAQ markup so Google can show the answer in the results. This is one of the few practice areas where the searcher’s question is almost always phrased as a question, which makes it a natural fit.
  • Plain-language guides: a downloadable checklist of documents to bring to the first meeting, or a simple guide to the filing process, earns links and bookmarks in a way a sales page never will.

Write for the person, not the algorithm: short sentences, plain words, and no jargon without an explanation. A page that reads like a statute will not be read at all.

7. Intake: fast, private, and judgment-free

Bankruptcy SEO brings the visitor. Intake decides whether that visitor becomes a client, and the gap between the two is where most firms lose cases they already paid to attract.

Three things matter most:

  • Speed. A person who has worked up the courage to contact a lawyer and hears nothing for two days will usually contact another firm — or decide to put the whole thing off again. Aim to respond within the hour during business hours, and say plainly when you will reply if the inquiry arrives outside them.
  • Privacy. Make it easy to reach you without being identified first — a short form, a phone number that is answered by a person, and a clear statement that the consultation is confidential. Some visitors will call from a work phone or a borrowed one; make that easy, not awkward.
  • Tone. The first conversation sets the relationship. No judgment, no surprise at the numbers, no pressure to file on the spot. Explain what happens next, what it will cost in general terms, and what the client should bring. People remember how the first call felt long after they forget what the website said.

Track every inquiry from first search to signed case, so you know which pages and which searches earn their keep — and which ones only produce traffic.

8. Measure signed cases, not traffic

Bankruptcy marketing is easy to misjudge, because traffic is a poor proxy for cases. A page about a celebrity bankruptcy may draw visitors who will never hire you. A Chapter 13 page with modest traffic that books consultations every week is worth far more.

Track, at minimum:

  • Calls and form submissions by page and by matter, so you know which pages earn their keep
  • Consultations booked — and consultations that become signed cases — by source and by matter
  • Cost per consultation and cost per signed case, if you run paid search alongside SEO
  • Time from first inquiry to filing, so you know how long to judge a page before changing it

Review those numbers monthly, not daily. Search visibility moves slowly, and a weekly panic about one ranking position leads to constant, unhelpful changes. A monthly review of signed cases per page tells you what to expand, what to rewrite, and what to stop funding.

9. The mistakes that cost bankruptcy firms clients

Most wasted bankruptcy SEO comes from a short list of avoidable errors:

  • One generic “Bankruptcy” page instead of a page per matter, so no page matches any specific search.
  • Hiding the frightening answers behind “contact us to discuss your situation” — the searcher leaves and asks Google instead.
  • Promising outcomes — “keep your house, guaranteed” and similar claims risk bar complaints and repel the careful clients you want most.
  • Ignoring the Map Pack, so the firm is invisible on the searches that produce the most calls.
  • A ten-field form that asks a private, stressed visitor for more than they are ready to give.
  • Slow intake, so the consultation goes to the firm that answered first.
  • Publishing client stories without proper consent, in a practice area where privacy is the whole point.
  • Judging SEO after a month, when rankings and trust build over quarters, not weeks.

Where to start this month

You do not need to rebuild everything at once. For most small-to-midsize bankruptcy firms, this order returns the most signed cases for the least spend:

  • This week: fix your Google Business Profile — bankruptcy named plainly, Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 listed as services, real photos, accurate hours — and shorten your contact form to the essentials
  • This month: build or rewrite your Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 pages around the frightening questions, add one location page for your strongest nearby city, and systematize review requests for after the filing
  • This quarter: publish one question-led article per week, answer every review, and review signed cases per page before spending more

Bankruptcy clients are not looking for the loudest firm in their city. They are looking for the firm that understands what prompted the search, answers the frightening question plainly, keeps the conversation private, and is still there — calm, local, and credible — when they are ready to decide. Be that firm in search, and your marketing will stop being a traffic exercise and start being a case pipeline.

Key Takeaways

  • Bankruptcy clients search in private, under real financial stress — your pages must lower the barrier, not raise it.
  • Rank for the searches that sign clients — Chapter 7, Chapter 13, stop foreclosure, stop garnishment — and answer the frightening questions directly.
  • Win the Map Pack: a clear bankruptcy profile, accurate details, and a steady flow of recent reviews.
  • One page per matter, a short form, and a visible phone number — never hide the answers behind a contact form.
  • Protect client privacy absolutely, and ask for reviews only after the filing, when the client can breathe again.
  • Judge SEO on signed cases per page, reviewed monthly — not on traffic.
  • Start with the profile, the Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 pages, and fast, judgment-free intake — then expand what proves itself.
