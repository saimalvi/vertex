Employment Law Firm Website Design: How to Turn Wrongful Termination Searches Into Consultations

VL
Vertex Law MarketingPublished 9 November 2026 · 10 min read

An employment law client rarely arrives at a law firm website calmly. They have usually just been fired, demoted, passed over, harassed, or underpaid — and they are searching while the situation is still raw. Often they are still employed, which means they are searching on a personal phone, outside work hours, and worrying about who might find out.

That changes what a website has to do. For a personal injury firm, the site has to prove results. For an employment firm, the site has to prove two things at once: that the visitor’s situation may actually be a legal problem, and that talking to a lawyer is safe.

The employment firms that sign the most clients from their websites are not the ones with the flashiest homepages. They are the ones whose sites help a stressed, possibly still-employed visitor understand their options quickly — and make the first step feel low-risk.

Here is how that kind of site is built.

1. Start with the visitor’s situation, not the firm’s history

Most law firm websites open with the firm: its history, its awards, its lawyers. An employment visitor skims past all of that, because none of it answers their question.

The first screen should answer three things within seconds:

  • Do you handle my situation? Name the problem plainly — wrongful termination, workplace discrimination, sexual harassment, wage theft, retaliation, disability accommodation — not “employment law” in general.
  • Is this actually illegal? Most visitors do not know whether what happened to them breaks the law. A short, honest explainer on the first screen — “not every unfair firing is illegal, but these kinds are” — earns trust immediately.
  • What happens if I contact you? One sentence: a confidential conversation, no obligation, and no contact with the employer unless the visitor decides to move forward.

Everything else — attorney biographies, office photos, credentials — belongs further down the page, after the visitor has decided the firm might be able to help. Our guide to website design that converts covers the conversion structure in detail; for employment, the order of that structure matters even more, because the visitor is deciding whether their problem is real and whether asking for help is safe.

2. Help visitors recognize a real claim — without diagnosing their case

The hardest part of employment marketing is that most visitors arrive unsure. They know something felt wrong, but they do not know if it was unlawful. A website that only says “we handle wrongful termination” loses them. A website that explains, in plain language, what actually counts as wrongful termination keeps them.

Practical rules for this content:

  • Name the situations people actually search for. Wrongful termination, discrimination based on race, sex, age, or disability, sexual harassment, unpaid overtime, misclassification as a contractor, retaliation for reporting something, and failure to accommodate a disability or pregnancy.
  • Explain the line between unfair and unlawful. Most states allow at-will employment, which means an employer can fire someone for a bad reason — but not for an illegal one. Say that plainly, with examples: fired for your race, sex, or age is illegal; fired because your manager disliked you usually is not.
  • Use the visitor’s words. People search “fired for no reason,” “boss cut my hours after I complained,” and “not paid overtime.” A page titled with legal jargon will not match those searches; a page that uses the words the visitor typed will.
  • Never promise an outcome. “You have a case” is a conclusion a lawyer reaches after hearing the facts, not a claim a website can make. Say what you will do — listen, review the facts, explain the options honestly — and let the consultation carry the rest.
  • Add a simple self-check. A short list of questions — were you fired shortly after complaining, requesting leave, or reporting something? — helps a visitor decide whether a consultation is worth their time, and it filters out the inquiries that will never become cases.

This content does double duty: it ranks for the long, specific searches employment clients actually type, and it pre-answers the uncertainty that stops people from calling.

3. Confidentiality is the design brief

An employment client carries a fear other legal clients usually do not: that contacting a lawyer could cost them the job they still have, or that their employer will find out. Many visitors are searching from a work device or a shared phone, and some are still deciding whether to act at all.

Your website has to answer that fear before it asks for anything. That means:

  • Say the consultation is confidential, and say it early. Put it near the form and the phone number, not only in a footer nobody reads. Visitors who are still employed need to hear it before they share a name.
  • Be specific about who you are. Real photos of the attorneys and staff, real names, real office address, and a real phone number. Stock photos of handshakes and skylines tell a visitor nothing — and in this practice area, anonymity reads as a warning sign.
  • Explain what happens after they contact you. Who will call back, when, and what they will ask. A visitor who knows the next step is a short, private conversation is far more likely to take it than one who imagines a lawsuit starting the moment they fill out a form.
  • Never ask for sensitive detail up front. Pay stubs, termination letters, and employer names belong in the consultation, not in a web form. A form that asks for them signals a firm that does not understand the stakes.
  • Offer a way to reach you quietly. Some visitors cannot talk freely where they are. A text or messaging option, with a clear note about when you will reply, reaches people a phone call cannot.

Trust is also why reviews need handling with care: never publish a client’s story, even anonymized, without proper consent, and never reply to a review in a way that reveals anything about a matter.

4. Design for the phone in their hand

Most employment searches happen on a mobile phone — during a lunch break, on the commute home, or late at night after a bad day at work. That is not a guess about the future; it is how people who cannot search from a work computer search today.

So design mobile-first, and test on a real phone, not only in a desktop browser:

  • A click-to-call button that stays visible. Many visitors would rather call than type, especially when the situation is emotional. Make calling one tap, on every page, at every scroll position.
  • A contact form with as few fields as possible. Name, phone or email, and one line about the situation. Nothing else at this stage — no employer details, no document uploads, no dates.
  • Text that is actually readable. Large type, generous line spacing, and strong contrast. If a visitor has to pinch and zoom, they will leave — and they will not come back.
  • Pages that load fast on a slow connection. Heavy images, video backgrounds, and chat widgets that load before the content all cost you visitors. A page that loads in two seconds on a weak signal earns the consultation; a page that takes eight seconds loses it.
  • A “browse privately” mindset. Assume the visitor may be on a shared device. Do not force account creation, do not auto-play video, and do not make the contact form the only way to get basic information.

This is where website design and employment marketing meet: a page that ranks first but is unusable on the phone that found it has wasted the ranking.

5. Make the first step feel safe

The consultation is the conversion for an employment firm. Everything on the site should make booking one feel easy and safe — and everything that makes it feel risky should be removed.

What works:

  • Say what the consultation covers and what it costs. If the first consultation is free, say so plainly. If it has a fee, say what it includes. Uncertainty about cost is one of the biggest reasons people do not call.
  • Offer a choice of contact method. Phone, form, or message — and let the visitor choose how and when you reply, so someone still employed can ask for an evening call.
  • Explain contingency fees in plain language. Many employment cases are taken on contingency, but most visitors do not know what that means. A short, honest paragraph — you pay nothing up front, the firm is paid from a recovery if there is one — removes one of the biggest barriers to calling.
  • Set a response expectation, and meet it. “We reply within one business day” is only useful if it is true. A visitor who hears nothing for three days will assume the worst and contact another firm.
  • Say what you will not do. “We will not contact your employer without your permission” is one sentence, and for a still-employed visitor it may be the most reassuring sentence on the whole site.

A short, honest explanation of what happens after the visitor contacts you turns an uncertain first step into an easy one.

6. Answer the questions they ask before they call

An employment visitor arrives with the same questions almost every time: Can I be fired for no reason? How long do I have to file a claim? What is my case worth? Do I have to pay up front? Will my employer find out? What if I signed a severance agreement?

Your site should answer those questions honestly, in plain language, on the pages where they arise. That means:

  • Short, clear guides for each matter you handle — what the process looks like, what affects timing, what is usually needed
  • A genuine frequently-asked-questions section on each practice page, written from real intake questions, not from keyword tools alone
  • Clear statements about fees in general terms, and about what a consultation does and does not include
  • An honest note where the answer genuinely depends on the facts — deadlines vary by claim and by state, and saying so is more useful than a single number

Keep the line between general information and legal advice visible: a page can explain how a wrongful termination claim usually works without telling a reader what will happen in their case.

7. Build one page per problem, not one page for employment law

“Employment lawyer” is a useful keyword, but it is also broad, competitive, and vague. A visitor searching for help with a specific situation — fired after reporting harassment, denied overtime pay, passed over after a pregnancy announcement — wants a page that describes their situation, not a general page about employment law.

Build one clear, genuine page for each matter clients actually search for:

  • Wrongful termination
  • Workplace discrimination
  • Sexual harassment and hostile work environment
  • Wage and hour claims — unpaid overtime, missed breaks, misclassification
  • Retaliation for reporting, whistleblowing, or taking leave
  • Disability and pregnancy accommodations
  • Severance review and negotiation

Each page should explain the problem in the visitor’s words, what the law generally says, what the process looks like, and what the first consultation covers — with the city and courts you serve named naturally. Resist the temptation to publish near-identical pages with only the city name changed: one strong page per matter, with genuine local detail, beats ten copied pages, and search engines treat thin, duplicated location pages harshly.

8. Deadlines and urgency — without pressure tactics

Employment claims have deadlines, and many visitors do not know it. Some claims must be filed with an agency within months, not years. That urgency is real, and your website should state it honestly — but there is a line between informing and pressuring.

  • State deadlines in general terms. “Many employment claims have filing deadlines measured in months, and missing one can end a claim entirely” is honest and useful. A countdown timer on the homepage is not.
  • Explain the agency step where it applies. In many states, a discrimination claim goes to an agency before it goes to court. A short explanation of that step shows the visitor the firm knows the process — and that acting early matters.
  • Do not manufacture urgency. “Call now before it’s too late” on every page repels the careful clients you want most. State the facts, explain the next step, and let the visitor decide.
  • Make the timeline visible. A simple step-by-step — consultation, review of documents, filing, negotiation or litigation — helps a visitor picture what happens next. People fear what they cannot picture; a clear timeline lowers the fear.

Urgency that comes from facts builds trust. Urgency that comes from a sales script destroys it.

9. Measure consultations, not traffic

Employment marketing is easy to misjudge, because traffic is a poor proxy for cases. A blog post about a celebrity wrongful termination lawsuit may draw visitors who will never hire you. A wage-claims page with modest traffic that books consultations every week is worth far more.

Track, at minimum:

  • Calls and form submissions by page, so you know which matter pages earn their keep
  • Consultations booked — and consultations that become clients — by source and by matter
  • Response time from first inquiry to first contact, by channel
  • Which questions and self-check content produce the most qualified consultations, so you know what to expand

Review those numbers monthly, not daily. Search visibility moves slowly, and a weekly panic about one ranking position leads to constant, unhelpful changes. A monthly review of consultations per page tells you what to expand, what to rewrite, and what to stop publishing.

Where to start this month

You do not need to rebuild everything at once. For most small-to-midsize employment firms, this order returns the most signed cases for the least spend:

  • This week: make your phone number one tap away on every mobile page, shorten your contact form to the essentials, and add a clear confidentiality note — including “we will not contact your employer without your permission” — beside it
  • This month: rebuild your top three matter pages — wrongful termination, discrimination, and wage claims — with plain-language explanations, the unfair-versus-unlawful line, and a clear consultation offer
  • This quarter: add real photos of your team, systematise review requests and fast responses, and review consultations per page before spending more on traffic

Employment clients are not looking for the loudest firm in their city. They are looking for the firm that understands what prompted the search, explains whether the problem may be a legal one, keeps the conversation private, and answers when it matters. Be that firm on your website, and your marketing will stop being a traffic exercise and start being a case pipeline.

Key Takeaways

  • Employment visitors search while stressed and often still employed. Confidentiality and clarity come before selling.
  • Help visitors recognize a real claim: explain the line between unfair and unlawful in their own words, without diagnosing their case.
  • Trust is the design brief: real people, a real office, an honest explanation of the process, and no promised outcomes.
  • Design mobile-first: one-tap calling, a short form, a messaging option, and pages that load fast on a weak signal.
  • Never ask for sensitive detail before the first conversation. Say plainly that you will not contact the employer without permission.
  • One genuine page per problem — wrongful termination, discrimination, harassment, wage claims, retaliation — beats one general employment page.
  • Judge the site by consultations booked per page and per matter, not by traffic.
