Estate Planning Law Firm SEO: How to Win Will, Trust, and Probate Searches in Your City

VL
Vertex Law MarketingPublished 13 November 2026 · 10 min read

An estate planning client rarely arrives at a law firm website in a hurry. They arrive when life forces the question — a new baby, a parent’s illness, a house to protect, a diagnosis, a second marriage, a business to pass on. They search quietly, often late at night, and they compare firms carefully before they call anyone.

That changes what SEO has to do. For most practice areas, ranking first is the hard part. For estate planning, ranking first is only the start: the visitor then has to decide, from a screen, that this firm is the one to trust with their family’s future. The firms that win the most estate planning work from search are not the ones with the most pages. They are the ones whose pages answer the real question behind the search — in plain language, with honest pricing signals, and with a clear next step.

Here is how that kind of visibility is built.

1. Start with the life event, not the firm’s credentials

Most estate planning websites open with the firm: years in practice, bar admissions, professional memberships. A visitor who just had a baby or just lost a parent skims past all of that, because none of it answers their question.

The first thing a visitor needs to know is whether the firm handles their situation:

  • Do you handle what I need? Name the situation plainly — a simple will, a revocable living trust, probate after a death, a power of attorney, guardianship for minor children, an advance directive — not “estate planning” in general.
  • How much will it cost? Most visitors assume a lawyer is expensive and put the call off for months. A page that explains, in general terms, how the firm charges — flat fees for common documents, what a consultation covers — removes the biggest reason people delay.
  • What happens first? A short, plain explanation of the first step — a conversation, a review of what they own and who matters to them, then a draft to review — turns an intimidating process into something a visitor can picture.

Everything else — attorney biographies, office photos, credentials — belongs further down the page, after the visitor has decided the firm might be able to help. 2. Build one page per service, not one page for estate planning

“Estate planning lawyer” is a useful keyword, but it is also broad, competitive, and vague. A visitor searching for help with a specific need — a will after a divorce, a trust for a special-needs child, probate for a parent’s estate — wants a page that describes their situation, not a general page about estate planning law.

Build one clear, genuine page for each service clients actually search for:

  • Wills — including simple wills, wills for parents of young children, and updating a will after marriage, divorce, or a move
  • Revocable living trusts — and the honest question of whether a trust or a will fits better
  • Probate and estate administration — what the process looks like, how long it usually takes, and what the executor has to do
  • Powers of attorney and health care proxies
  • Advance directives and living wills
  • Guardianship designations for minor children
  • Business succession planning for owners of small businesses
  • Planning for blended families and second marriages

Each page should explain the service in the visitor’s words, what the process looks like, what it generally costs, and what the first consultation covers — with the city and courts you serve named naturally. Resist the temptation to publish near-identical pages with only the city name changed: one strong page per service, with genuine local detail, beats ten copied pages, and search engines treat thin, duplicated location pages harshly.

3. Win the local searches, because estate planning is a local decision

Almost nobody hires an estate planning lawyer from another state. They search with their city in mind, and they choose a firm they can visit, call, and meet in person. That makes local SEO the highest-value work an estate planning firm can do.

The local foundation is the same one every practice area needs, and it is worth doing properly:

  • A complete, accurate Google Business Profile. Correct name, address, and phone number, the right categories, real photos of the office and the team, and hours that match the website. Inconsistencies between the profile and the site cost rankings and trust.
  • Service pages that name the city naturally. “Wills and trusts in [City]” should read like a page written for people in that city, with local courts, local probate procedures, and local detail — not a keyword stuffed into a generic template.
  • Reviews that mention the service, not just the firm. A review that says “helped us set up a trust after our daughter was born” tells the next visitor far more than “great lawyer.” Ask clients, after the work is done, to describe what the firm did for them.
  • Consistent listings everywhere else. Directories, bar association listings, and local business listings should all carry the same name, address, and phone number. Small mismatches add up.

Local visibility compounds: a firm that ranks in the map pack for wills also becomes easier to find for trusts, probate, and powers of attorney, because Google already trusts it as the local firm for this work.

4. Answer the cost question honestly, or visitors will keep searching

The single biggest reason people delay estate planning is the fear of the cost — and the second biggest is the fear of being sold something they do not need. A website that dodges the cost question loses both kinds of visitor to the firm that answers it.

  • Explain how you charge, in general terms. Many estate planning firms charge flat fees for common documents. Say so, and explain what a flat fee usually covers — the consultation, the draft, the signing meeting, and the copies — and what would change the price.
  • Give ranges, not promises. A general range for a simple will or a revocable living trust, with a clear note that the exact fee depends on the facts, is honest and useful. A single fixed number that does not hold up in the consultation destroys trust faster than no number at all.
  • Say what the consultation costs. If the first consultation is free, say so plainly, and say what it covers. If it is paid, say that too — and explain what the visitor gets for it. Surprises about fees are the fastest way to lose a client who was ready to hire you.
  • Never promise an outcome. “We will protect your assets” is a conclusion that depends on the facts, the law, and the documents. Say what you will do — listen, review the situation, explain the options honestly, and draft documents that match the plan — and let the consultation carry the rest.
  • Address the DIY question directly. Many visitors arrive wondering whether an online form would do. An honest page explains, in plain language, where a simple online will can go wrong — witnesses, signing requirements, state-specific rules, and what happens when a document does not match the plan — without frightening anyone. That honesty is what turns a researcher into a caller.

Cost clarity is also why a short intake form matters: name, phone or email, and one line about what they need. Asking for a full inventory of assets before the first conversation signals a firm that does not understand the visitor’s situation.

5. Write for the questions people actually type

Estate planning searches are questions, not keywords. People type what they are worried about, in their own words — and the firms that answer those questions in plain language win both the ranking and the call.

The questions come up in almost every intake, so the answers should already be on the site:

  • How much does a will cost, and what does it cover?
  • Do I need a trust or a will — and what is the difference?
  • What happens if someone dies without a will in my state?
  • How long does probate take, and can it be avoided?
  • Who should be the executor, and what does an executor actually do?
  • How do I choose a guardian for my children?
  • What is a power of attorney, and when does it take effect?
  • How often should I update my estate plan?

Answer each question on the page where it arises, in short paragraphs a non-lawyer can follow, with the honest qualifier where the answer genuinely depends on the facts or the state. A genuine frequently-asked-questions section on each service page, written from real intake questions rather than keyword tools alone, does more for rankings and for trust than another generic article about estate planning.

Keep the line between general information and legal advice visible: a page can explain how probate usually works without telling a reader what will happen in their case. That distinction is not just good marketing — it is the right thing to say, and careful visitors notice when a firm says it.

6. Prove trust without inventing anything

Estate planning is the practice area where trust matters most and is hardest to earn. A visitor is choosing the firm that will hold their family’s most personal decisions — who raises their children, who makes medical choices, who inherits the house. They arrive skeptical, and they notice everything.

  • Show real people. Real photos of the attorneys and staff, real names, a real office address, and a real phone number. Stock photos of handshakes and skylines tell a visitor nothing — and in this practice area, anonymity reads as a warning sign.
  • Describe experience in general terms. The types of plans handled, the courts served, and how the firm works through a plan are all honest, useful proof. Specific past results can only be shown where the firm’s jurisdiction allows it, and only with the figures the firm can actually support.
  • Handle reviews with care. Real reviews, shown in full, build trust. Never publish a client’s story, even anonymized, without proper consent, and never reply to a review in a way that reveals anything about a matter.
  • Explain the firm’s role honestly. Many visitors do not know what an estate planning lawyer actually does — listening, mapping the family and the assets, explaining the options, drafting documents that match the plan, and keeping the plan current. A short, plain explanation of that work is more convincing than any slogan.
  • Keep advertising rules visible. Every state has its own rules for attorney advertising. What the site says about results, fees, and specialization has to comply with those rules, not just with marketing instinct.

7. Turn one plan into the next client

Estate planning has something most practice areas do not: a natural referral engine. A client who has just finished a will talks about it — to a sibling, a parent, a friend who just had a baby, a financial advisor, an accountant. The firms that grow fastest from search are the ones that make that second step easy.

  • Ask for reviews at the right moment. After the signing meeting, when the client is relieved and grateful, a short request — “would you share a few words about your experience?” — works far better than a request sent months later. One prompt ask, then leave it alone.
  • Make referrals easy, not awkward. A simple line at the end of the process — that the firm welcomes introductions to family members who need the same help — is enough. Clients who had a good experience want to help the people they love; the firm only has to make it easy.
  • Stay in touch after the plan is signed. A short, useful check-in — a reminder to review the plan after a move, a marriage, a birth, or a death in the family — keeps the firm top of mind and brings clients back when life changes. It also produces the updates that keep a plan valid.
  • Build relationships with the advisors clients already trust. Financial advisors, accountants, and insurance professionals meet people at exactly the moment they need an estate plan. A firm that is easy to refer to — clear services, clear pricing signals, a website that reassures — gets those referrals.

Referrals do not replace search; they multiply it. A visitor who finds the firm on Google and then hears a friend mention the same firm is far more likely to call than a visitor who only saw the ranking.

8. State differences matter — say so

Estate planning law is state law. What a will requires in one state, how probate works in another, and whether a trust avoids probate at all all depend on where the client lives. A website that pretends the answers are the same everywhere loses the trust of the careful visitors a firm wants most.

  • Name the state and the courts you serve. Not as a keyword, but as a fact: the probate court, the signing and witness requirements, and the local procedures the firm handles every week.
  • Flag where the answer varies. Where the law genuinely differs by state, say so plainly and explain what the firm’s state requires. Visitors who live in that state will recognize their own situation; visitors from elsewhere will understand the firm is not for them, which is also useful.
  • Do not give specific advice on a page. General information about how a trust usually works is useful. Telling a reader what they should do with their house is not the website’s job — it is the consultation’s.
  • Keep the content current. Tax thresholds, exemption amounts, and probate rules change. A page that still quotes an old figure tells a visitor the firm is not paying attention. Review the service pages at least once a year, and date the review.

Honesty about state differences is a ranking advantage as well as a trust advantage: pages that describe a real local process rank better for local searches than pages that could have been written for anywhere.

9. Measure consultations, not traffic

Estate planning marketing is easy to misjudge, because traffic is a poor proxy for clients. A blog post about a celebrity’s estate may draw visitors who will never hire you. A wills page with modest traffic that books consultations every week is worth far more.

Track, at minimum:

  • Calls and form submissions by page, so you know which service pages earn their keep
  • Consultations booked — and consultations that become signed engagements — by source and by service
  • Response time from first inquiry to first contact, by channel
  • Cost per signed engagement by channel, so you know what the website, the ads, and the map listing each actually deliver

Review those numbers monthly, not daily. Search visibility moves slowly, and a weekly panic about one ranking position leads to constant, unhelpful changes. A monthly review of consultations per page tells you what to expand, what to rewrite, and what to stop publishing.

Where to start this month

You do not need to rebuild everything at once. For most small-to-midsize estate planning firms, this order returns the most consultations for the least spend:

  • This week: make your phone number one tap away on every mobile page, shorten your contact form to the essentials, and put the consultation offer and a plain explanation of your fees beside it
  • This month: rebuild your top three service pages — wills, revocable living trusts, and probate — with plain-language explanations, the consultation offer, and a clear first step
  • This quarter: add real photos of your team, systematise review requests after signing meetings, and review consultations per page before spending more on traffic

Estate planning clients are not looking for the loudest firm in their city. They are looking for the firm that understands what just happened in their life, explains what it costs in plain language, and makes the first step feel easy. Be that firm in search, and your marketing will stop being a traffic exercise and start being a client pipeline.

Key Takeaways

  • Estate planning clients search when life forces the question. The first screen must name their situation, explain the cost in general terms, and show the first step.
  • One genuine page per service — wills, trusts, probate, powers of attorney, guardianship — beats one general estate planning page.
  • Estate planning is a local decision. A complete Google Business Profile, city-specific service pages, and reviews that mention the service win the searches that matter.
  • Cost is the biggest reason people delay. Explain flat fees and consultation terms honestly, and never promise an outcome.
  • Answer the questions people actually type — will or trust, what happens without a will, how long probate takes — in plain language, on the page where they arise.
  • Trust is the design brief: real people, a real office, honest general information, and no invented results.
  • Judge the work by consultations per page and per service, not by traffic.
