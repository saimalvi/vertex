Bankruptcy Law Firm Website Design: How to Turn Debt-Relief Searches Into Signed Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 Cases

VL
Vertex Law MarketingPublished 27 November 2026 · 10 min read

A bankruptcy client is not browsing. They are sitting up late, on a phone, with bills they cannot pay and calls they cannot answer, typing “can I keep my house if I file bankruptcy” or “bankruptcy lawyer near me” and hoping the answer is not as frightening as they expect. They are embarrassed. They are afraid of being judged. And they are deciding, right now, whether to call anyone at all.

That is why most bankruptcy websites fail. They are built like every other law firm site: a stock photo of a courthouse, a list of practice areas, a contact form — and nothing that speaks to the person who is quietly panicking at 1 a.m. The firms that sign bankruptcy clients online are the ones whose websites meet the visitor where they actually are: stressed, private, and one bad experience away from closing the tab.

Here is how bankruptcy firms design websites that turn Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 searches into signed cases.

1. Lead with reassurance, not credentials

Most bankruptcy websites open with the firm: years in practice, bar admissions, a mission statement. A visitor who is behind on a mortgage and fielding calls from collectors does not care about any of that yet. They care about two questions: will I lose my house, and will I be judged for this?

Lead with the answers to those fears, in plain language. The hero section should say, in the visitor’s own words, what the firm helps with — stopping creditor calls, protecting a home and a car, wiping out credit card and medical debt, giving a family a fresh start — and that the first consultation is free and confidential. A headline like “Stop the calls. Keep your home. Start over.” does more work than any list of credentials, because it tells the visitor the firm understands exactly why they are there.

The firm’s experience still matters, but it belongs further down the page, after the visitor has decided the site will not judge them. Trust in bankruptcy is built in this order: you understand what I am going through, you will not judge me, and only then, you know how to fix it.

2. Answer the two questions every visitor is afraid to ask

Every bankruptcy visitor has the same two fears, and most bankruptcy websites answer neither of them. The first is “will I lose my house and my car?” The second is “how much will this cost me, when I already have no money?”

Answer both on the homepage, in plain language. Explain that most people who file Chapter 7 keep their home and their car, because exemption laws protect them — and that the firm will check the visitor’s specific situation before they file anything. Explain that Chapter 13 is designed for people who want to keep property and pay back what they can afford over three to five years. And be honest about fees: explain whether Chapter 7 is usually handled on a flat fee, whether Chapter 13 fees are often paid through the repayment plan, and that the first consultation is free and the firm will give a clear estimate before any work begins.

You do not need to publish a price list, and in most cases you should not. But a site that says nothing about cost leaves a visitor who is already broke to assume the worst, and they leave. A page that explains how fees work removes the barrier that stops most people from calling.

3. Build one page per chapter and per problem, not one page for bankruptcy

A single “Bankruptcy” page that mentions Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 in a few sentences each serves nobody: it is too thin to rank, too vague to reassure, and too generic to convert. Bankruptcy firms that win online give each problem its own page.

Each page should be written for the person who has that problem. A Chapter 7 page should explain what debts it wipes out, what it does not, who qualifies, and how long the process takes. A Chapter 13 page should explain the repayment plan, who it is for, and what it means for a mortgage that is behind. A foreclosure page should explain the automatic stay and the deadline a homeowner is facing. A page for medical debt should speak to the family whose hospital bills became unpayable.

  • Chapter 7 — what it wipes out, what it does not, who qualifies, and how fast it works
  • Chapter 13 — the repayment plan, keeping a home and a car, and who it suits
  • Stopping foreclosure — the automatic stay, the deadlines, and what to do first
  • Stopping creditor calls and lawsuits — what the automatic stay does, and what to do about a garnishment
  • Medical debt and credit card debt — when the bills became unpayable, and what bankruptcy can do
  • Small business debt — options for a business owner whose debts have become personal

Each page should name the city and the bankruptcy court you serve naturally, explain the process in the visitor’s words, and end with the same clear next step: a free, confidential consultation. One strong page per problem, with genuine local detail, beats a dozen thin pages — and search engines treat near-identical pages with only the city name changed harshly. 4. Design for the 1 a.m. visitor on a phone

Bankruptcy clients search late, on a phone, often from a couch or a car — and often in private, because they have not told their spouse or their family yet. The website has to work perfectly in those conditions.

Make the call button big and always visible. A visitor who has worked up the courage to call should never have to hunt for the number, and a tap-to-call button that works with one thumb is worth more than any amount of copy. Make the consultation form short enough to finish in the dark: a name, a way to reach them, and a short message box where they can write “behind on mortgage, getting calls” in their own words. And make the site fast — a page that loads in a couple of seconds keeps a stressed visitor, and a heavy page full of oversized images does not.

Just as important is privacy. A bankruptcy visitor is often worried about who will know. Say plainly on the site that consultations are confidential, that the firm will never share their information, and that they do not need to tell anyone they called. A site that respects the visitor’s privacy earns a call that a careless site never gets.

5. Explain the process before you ask for the call

Bankruptcy clients delay because they do not know what the process involves. They imagine losing everything, going to court in front of a judge, and having their finances picked apart in public. A website that explains the process step by step removes that fear.

Show the visitor exactly what happens. A simple outline works: first, a free consultation where the attorney listens and explains the options; second, a review of the debts, income, and property to see which chapter fits; third, the filing, which triggers the automatic stay and stops the calls; and finally, the meeting of creditors and the discharge. A short paragraph for each step, in plain language, tells the visitor the process is manageable — and that the firm will guide them through it.

Be honest about the parts clients worry about most: that in most cases there is no courtroom appearance before a judge, that the meeting of creditors is usually brief and straightforward, and that the firm handles the paperwork. The site that explains the scary parts in plain language is the site the visitor trusts.

6. Prove trust honestly — real people, real process

Bankruptcy is a trust business, and it is also a shame business. A visitor is choosing the person they will tell their worst financial secrets to. They need to believe the lawyer is competent, careful, and kind. A website proves that with real people and a real process, not with invented results or borrowed authority.

Attorney pages should feel like people, not résumés. A real photo, a plain-language explanation of what the attorney focuses on, and a short note on how they approach bankruptcy — listening first, explaining in plain English, never judging a client’s situation — tells a visitor far more than a list of bar admissions. If an attorney has genuine training that matters to clients, say so. If not, do not invent it.

The same honesty rule applies to reviews and results. Genuine client reviews, shown with the client’s own words and attributed properly, are powerful. Fabricated testimonials, invented statistics, and outcome guarantees are not — they mislead visitors, they breach advertising rules in many states, and they destroy trust the moment a client discovers the gap. If the firm is new and has few reviews, that is fine: explain the process, show the people, and let the consultation do the convincing. 7. A consultation form that feels safe, not intrusive

The consultation form is where a bankruptcy website wins or loses the client, and most firms make it far too hard. A visitor who is behind on their bills does not want to fill in a long form asking about their income, their debts, and their assets before they have even spoken to a lawyer. A form that demands all of that will be abandoned halfway through.

Ask for three things at the first step and nothing else: a name, a way to reach them, and what they need help with — a short, free-text box where they can write “getting sued by a collector” or “house is in foreclosure” in their own words. That is enough for the firm to call back with useful help. Everything else — the debts, the income, the property — belongs in the consultation itself, not in a form.

Make the form work on a phone with a thumb, label every field clearly, and confirm the submission in plain language: “Thank you — we have received your message and will call you back within one business day.” Better still, say what happens next: who will call, when, and that the call is free and confidential. And test the form regularly — a form that silently fails to send is a hole in the bottom of the firm’s pipeline that nobody notices until the month’s numbers come in.

8. Speak to the spouse, not just the person who searches

Bankruptcy decisions are rarely made alone. Often one spouse does the searching and the other needs convincing. Often the person in debt has not told their partner yet, and the website is the first place they are saying it out loud. Your website should speak to both of them.

That means writing for the partner as well as the searcher. A page that says “if you are looking into bankruptcy for your family, here is what to expect, and here is how the first meeting works” speaks directly to a large share of your real visitors. It also means acknowledging the emotions involved: shame, fear, and relief, all at once — and a site that treats them with warmth and plain language will be trusted far more than one that treats them as transactions.

Consider a simple downloadable guide — “what to bring to your first bankruptcy consultation” or “five things to know before you file” — offered in exchange for an email address. It is genuinely useful, it gives the visitor a reason to stay in touch, and it starts the relationship before the consultation. Keep the ask small: a name and an email address, nothing more.

9. Local pages that actually help

Bankruptcy is a local business: clients want a lawyer who knows their bankruptcy court, their trustee, and their state’s exemption laws. The website should show that local knowledge, but with genuine detail rather than duplicated pages.

A location or “serving” page for each city or county the firm covers should include real local information: which bankruptcy court handles cases there, how the state’s homestead and vehicle exemptions generally work, and any state-specific rules that affect what a client keeps. That is the kind of detail a visitor recognizes as genuine, and it is the kind of detail search engines reward.

What does not work is publishing near-identical pages for twenty towns with only the town name changed. Thin, duplicated location pages do not rank, and they make the firm look like a directory rather than a local practice. One honest, detailed page per place you genuinely serve beats a wall of copy-and-paste. 10. Measure signed cases, not traffic

A bankruptcy website can get more traffic every month and still sign fewer clients, if the traffic is the wrong kind or the site does not convert it. The number to watch is not visits — it is consultations booked, and consultations that become signed Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 cases.

Track the basics properly: how many consultation requests the site produces each month, which pages those requests come from, how many become signed clients, and how fast the firm responds. A foreclosure page that brings in ten consultations a month is worth more than a blog post that brings in a thousand visitors and no calls. Bankruptcy clients decide fast once they call, but they may read the site for days before they do — so track them over a longer window, and credit the website when a visitor who read in November calls in December.

That measurement is also what makes the next round of improvements obvious. If the Chapter 7 page converts and the Chapter 13 page does not, the Chapter 13 page is the one to rewrite. If mobile visitors request consultations at half the rate of desktop visitors, the mobile experience is the problem. A website is not finished when it launches; it is finished when the numbers say it is working. Where to start this month

If the firm’s site needs work, do not try to rebuild everything at once. Start with the changes that matter most to a bankruptcy visitor.

  • This week: make the tap-to-call button big and always visible, cut the consultation form down to a name, a contact, and a short message, and add a plain-language promise that consultations are free and confidential.
  • This month: give Chapter 7, Chapter 13, and foreclosure each their own page with honest answers to the questions clients actually ask, and add a plain-language explanation of fees and the first meeting.
  • This quarter: add genuine local pages for the cities and counties you serve, add a simple downloadable guide for visitors who are not ready to call yet, and start tracking signed cases per page over a longer decision window.

Bankruptcy clients choose the firm that understands their fear, answers the questions they are afraid to ask, and makes the first step feel safe. A website that leads with reassurance, explains the process in plain language, protects the visitor’s privacy, and makes the consultation effortless will win those clients — and keep winning them, month after month.
