Google Ads for Family Law Attorneys: Turn Divorce, Custody, and Support Searches Into Signed Clients

VL
Vertex Law MarketingPublished 14 November 2026 · 10 min read

A family law client rarely searches the way other legal clients do. They search in private, often late at night, often on a phone they share with the person they are thinking of leaving. They are not browsing. They are deciding whether to pick up the phone — and if your ad is the one they see, you have seconds to convince them that this firm is safe to call.

That is what makes Google Ads different for family law. In most practice areas, the advertiser’s job is to be the most visible firm for a search. In family law, visibility is only the start: the click has to land on a page that understands the visitor’s situation, the call has to be answered by someone who knows how to handle it, and the budget has to survive the keywords that look promising and never convert.

Here is how family law firms run Google Ads that actually turn searches into signed clients.

1. Start with the searcher’s situation, not the firm’s services

Most family law ads read the same way: a firm name, a list of practice areas, and a phone number. A person searching “divorce lawyer near me” at 11 p.m. skims past all of that, because none of it answers the question they are actually asking: can this firm help with my situation, and is it safe to call?

Family law searches fall into a few situations, and each one needs its own message:

  • Ending a marriage. Divorce, contested or uncontested, separation, and annulment. The searcher wants to know what the process looks like, what it generally costs, and whether they can get advice before they tell their spouse.
  • Children. Custody, visitation, parenting plans, and relocation. The searcher is usually a parent who is afraid of losing time with their child. The ad and the page must speak to that fear directly and calmly.
  • Money. Child support, spousal support, and property division. The searcher wants numbers and clarity: how support is usually calculated, what counts as marital property, and what a consultation will tell them.
  • Protection. Restraining orders and domestic violence matters. The searcher may be in danger. The page must make the next step obvious and immediate — a phone number that is easy to find, and a clear statement that the firm can help quickly.
  • Changing an existing order. Modifications, enforcement, and contempt. The searcher already has an order and something has changed — a job loss, a move, a parent who is not following the plan.

One campaign that treats all of these as “family law” wastes budget on clicks that never had a chance. A campaign built around the situation, with its own ad copy and its own landing page, converts far better — and it tells the searcher, in the first three seconds, that this firm understands what is happening in their life. 2. Build one campaign per matter type, with its own landing page

The single most common mistake in family law Google Ads is the one-campaign account: a single campaign, a single ad group, a mixed bag of keywords, and every click sent to the homepage. It is easy to set up, and it burns budget steadily.

Separate the account by matter type, because each one behaves differently:

  • Divorce. High volume, high competition, and a long decision window. Many clicks are research, not intent. Expect a lower conversion rate and plan the budget accordingly.
  • Custody. Lower volume, but the searcher is often further along and more motivated. These clicks cost less than divorce clicks in many markets and convert at a higher rate.
  • Child support and spousal support. Often searched by people who already have a case or an order. The landing page should answer the calculation question in general terms and offer a consultation to apply it to their facts.
  • Restraining orders. Low volume, urgent, and time-sensitive. The landing page should not ask the visitor to read a long article. It should offer a call, immediately, and say what happens next.
  • Modifications. Smaller volume and very specific intent. These are often the cheapest clicks in the account and some of the best converting, because the searcher already knows what they need.

Each campaign gets its own landing page, and the page matches the ad. A person who clicked an ad about custody should land on a custody page — not the homepage, not a general family law page, and not a page that makes them scroll to find the one paragraph about their situation. The headline should repeat the searcher’s situation back to them, the first screen should name what the firm does about it, and the phone number should be visible without scrolling. Our guide to website design that converts covers the landing page structure in detail; for paid traffic, the structure matters even more, because you are paying for every visitor who bounces.

3. Choose keywords by intent, not by volume

Family law keywords are expensive, and the most expensive ones are often the least useful. “Divorce lawyer” in a major metro can cost far more per click than a phrase that signals a person ready to hire. The job is not to buy the most searches. It is to buy the searches most likely to become a consultation.

Sort every keyword by what the searcher is actually doing:

  • Ready to hire. “Divorce lawyer near me,” “custody attorney [city],” “family law attorney free consultation.” These are the keywords to bid on first and protect with the largest share of the budget.
  • Comparing options. “Best divorce lawyer [city],” “divorce attorney reviews.” Smaller volume, but the searcher is choosing a firm. These deserve their own ad group and a landing page that makes the choice easy — real people, real process, clear next step.
  • Researching a question. “How much does a divorce cost,” “who gets custody in [state],” “how is child support calculated.” These searches are valuable for organic content and for remarketing, but they are poor paid keywords: the searcher is not ready to call, and you will pay for the click anyway.
  • Do-it-yourself. “Free divorce forms,” “file for divorce online,” “do I need a lawyer for divorce.” These are the clicks to exclude. They cost money and almost never become clients.

Use phrase and exact match for the money keywords, and keep broad match on a short leash. Broad match in family law is how an account ends up paying for searches about celebrity divorces, divorce paperwork in another state, and legal aid the firm does not offer. Check the search terms report every week for the first month, and add negative keywords aggressively — free, forms, DIY, jobs, salary, law school, and the names of firms you do not want to appear against. Our guide to setting a Google Ads budget explains how to size the spend by practice area and market; for family law, the honest starting point is the cost per consultation, not the cost per click.

4. Write ads for a person in crisis, not for a search engine

Family law ad copy fails when it sounds like every other law firm ad: “Experienced. Aggressive. Call now.” A person going through a divorce or a custody fight does not want aggressive. They want a firm that sounds steady, discreet, and competent.

A few principles that work:

  • Name the situation in the headline. “Divorce Lawyer in [City]” and “Child Custody Attorney — [City]” outperform generic headlines, because the searcher sees their own situation reflected back. Keep the city in the ad where it fits naturally.
  • Offer a private, low-pressure next step. A confidential consultation, a free initial call, or a short conversation to understand the situation. The offer should feel safe, not like a sales pitch.
  • Say what the consultation covers. “Understand your options, the process, and what it generally costs — before you decide anything.” That sentence does more work than any slogan, because it answers the two questions every searcher has: what will this cost, and what happens if I call?
  • Use the description to reassure. Mention the firm’s focus on family law, the courts served, and that calls are handled discreetly. Avoid superlatives and promises about outcomes — they do not reassure a careful visitor, and they can create advertising problems.
  • Use ad assets properly. Call assets so the number is one tap away on mobile, location assets so the firm looks real and nearby, and sitelink assets that send the searcher straight to the page for their situation — divorce, custody, support — instead of the homepage.

Test two or three ad variations per ad group and let the data decide. The winning ad in family law is rarely the clever one. It is usually the plainest one — the one that names the situation, offers a private conversation, and makes the next step obvious.

5. The landing page decides whether the click was worth paying for

A click costs money the moment it happens. The landing page decides whether that money was spent or wasted. Most family law landing pages waste it, because they were built to describe the firm rather than to help the visitor decide.

The first screen of a family law landing page should do four things, and nothing else:

  • Repeat the situation. The headline should match the ad the visitor clicked. If the ad was about custody, the headline should be about custody.
  • Offer the consultation. A clear, calm offer — a free initial consultation, a private call — with the phone number visible and the form short.
  • Explain the first step. One or two sentences on what happens in the consultation: the firm listens, explains the options and the process, and gives a general sense of cost. No jargon, no legalese.
  • Make the call safe. A line that calls are confidential and discreet matters more in family law than in almost any other practice area. Many visitors are searching on a shared device or a shared phone plan, and the fear of being discovered is real.

Everything below that first screen is support: what the firm handles, how the process generally works, the questions people ask most often, and a short form. Keep the form to the essentials — name, phone or email, and one line about the situation. A form that asks for a full case history before the first conversation signals a firm that does not understand the visitor’s position.

Mobile deserves special attention. Most family law clicks come from phones, often late at night. The number should be one tap to call, the form should work with a thumb, and the page should load fast on a weak connection. A page that takes six seconds to load on a phone has already lost the visitor who was ready to call.

6. Answer the phone like the ad promised

Google Ads can deliver the click, and the landing page can deliver the call. What happens next is the part most firms never measure — and it is where family law accounts usually leak the most money.

A family law caller is often calling from a difficult moment. They may be emotional, they may be whispering, they may hang up if the call goes to voicemail. A few things make the difference:

  • Answer fast, every time. A missed call in family law is rarely a redial. If the firm cannot answer every call in person, an answering service that is trained on family law intake — calm, discreet, and able to take a message properly — pays for itself quickly.
  • Train intake for this practice area. The person answering should know how to listen without giving legal advice, how to ask the few questions that matter, and how to book the consultation without making the caller repeat their story to three different people.
  • Respond to forms within the hour. A form submitted at midnight deserves a response first thing in the morning, not two days later. Speed is not a nice-to-have in family law; it is often the deciding factor between your firm and the next one on the list.
  • Track the call, not just the click. Call tracking that ties each call back to the campaign and keyword that produced it is the only honest way to judge the account. Without it, the firm is guessing which keywords earn their keep.

7. Keep the account honest: negatives, geo-targeting, and the budget

Family law accounts drift. A keyword that converted last quarter stops converting. A new search term appears that has nothing to do with hiring a lawyer. A campaign starts spending in a city the firm does not serve. The accounts that perform are the ones that get maintained, not the ones that get launched and forgotten.

The weekly discipline is short:

  • Search terms report. Read it every week. Add negative keywords for anything that is not a person looking for a family law attorney in your area — forms, DIY, jobs, other states, legal aid, and celebrity news.
  • Geo-targeting. Target the cities and counties the firm actually serves, and check the geographic report for clicks from places outside them. A firm that only practices in one county should not be paying for clicks from three states away.
  • Device and time. Family law searches spike in the evenings and on weekends, when people are home and thinking. If the budget is limited, consider bidding more during the hours when calls are actually answered, and less when the office is closed and the phone goes to voicemail.
  • Budget by matter type. Divorce usually needs the largest budget and returns the slowest conversions. Custody and modifications often convert faster on a smaller spend. Review the cost per consultation by campaign, not the cost per click, and move budget toward the campaigns that produce consultations.
  • Pause what does not convert. A keyword with dozens of clicks and no consultations over a reasonable period is not “building awareness.” It is spending money. Pause it, and put the budget where the consultations are.

8. Advertising rules and the claims you must not make

Family law advertising is watched more closely than most legal advertising, because the audience is vulnerable and the stakes are personal. Every state has its own rules, and the ad copy has to comply with them, not just with marketing instinct.

  • Never promise an outcome. “We will win your custody case” is a conclusion that depends on the facts, the law, and the court. Say what the firm will do — listen, explain the options, prepare the case properly — and let the consultation carry the rest.
  • Be careful with “best” and “top” language. Superlatives need support. A firm can describe its focus, its experience in general terms, and the courts it serves. It should not claim a ranking it cannot substantiate.
  • Handle testimonials and reviews properly. Only show reviews the firm actually has, in full, and never publish a client’s story — even anonymized — without proper consent. A review reply that reveals anything about a matter is a serious mistake.
  • Keep the line between information and advice visible. An ad or a landing page can explain how custody is generally decided. It cannot tell a visitor what will happen in their case. That distinction is not just good marketing — it is the right thing to say, and careful visitors notice when a firm says it.
  • Check the platform’s rules as well as the bar’s. Google has its own policies for legal services advertising, and they change. An ad that was approved last year may not be approved today. Review the account’s policy status regularly, and fix disapprovals quickly rather than letting a campaign sit paused.

Honesty is also a conversion advantage. A family law visitor who has just been through a difficult conversation with their spouse is looking for a firm that sounds steady and straight. An ad that overpromises reads as a warning sign, not a reason to call.

9. Measure consultations and signed cases, not clicks

Family law Google Ads is easy to misjudge, because the numbers that look good are often the wrong ones. Impressions and clicks go up, the account looks busy, and the firm is no closer to signing clients. Judge the account by what happens after the click.

Track, at minimum:

  • Calls and form submissions by campaign and by keyword, so you know which searches earn their keep
  • Consultations booked — and consultations that become signed engagements — by campaign and by matter type
  • Cost per consultation and cost per signed engagement, not cost per click
  • Response time from first inquiry to first contact, by channel
  • Which landing pages convert and which ones visitors leave without calling

Review those numbers monthly, not daily. A single day’s data in family law is noise — a weekend spike, a slow Monday, a holiday. A monthly review of cost per consultation by campaign tells you what to expand, what to rewrite, and what to pause.

Where to start this month

You do not need to rebuild the whole account at once. For most small-to-midsize family law firms, this order returns the most consultations for the least spend:

  • This week: separate the account into divorce, custody, support, and protection campaigns, each with its own landing page; add the obvious negative keywords — free, forms, DIY, jobs — and make the phone number one tap away on every mobile page
  • This month: rebuild the top three landing pages — divorce, custody, and child support — with a headline that repeats the visitor’s situation, a short form, and a clear consultation offer; set up call tracking by campaign
  • This quarter: train intake for family law calls, set a response-time standard for forms, and review cost per consultation by campaign before increasing any budget

Family law clients are not looking for the loudest firm in their city. They are looking for the firm that understands what is happening in their life, answers the phone calmly, and makes the first step feel safe. Be that firm in the ad, on the page, and on the phone — and your Google Ads spend will stop being a traffic exercise and start being a client pipeline.

Key Takeaways

  • Family law clients search in private, under stress, and close to home. The ad, the landing page, and the phone call all have to feel safe.
  • Build one campaign per matter type — divorce, custody, support, protection, modifications — each with its own landing page that matches the ad.
  • Choose keywords by intent, not volume. Research and DIY searches are poor paid keywords; ready-to-hire and comparing searches are where the budget belongs.
  • Write ads for a person in crisis: name the situation, offer a private consultation, and make the next step obvious.
  • The landing page decides whether the click was worth paying for: repeat the situation, offer the consultation, explain the first step, and make the call safe.
  • Answer the phone like the ad promised — fast, calm, and discreet — and track calls back to the campaign that produced them.
  • Judge the account by cost per consultation and cost per signed engagement, not by clicks or impressions.
