Employment Law Firm SEO: How to Win Wrongful Termination, Discrimination, and Wage Claim Searches in Your City

VL
Vertex Law MarketingPublished 16 November 2026 · 10 min read

An employment client rarely arrives at a law firm website the way other clients do. They are not planning a purchase or researching a life event. Something went wrong at work — a firing that felt unfair, a demotion after a complaint, pay that never arrived, a comment that crossed a line — and now they are searching on a phone, often during a lunch break, often while still employed, wondering whether what happened to them was unlawful and whether they can afford to do anything about it.

That changes what SEO has to do. For most practice areas, ranking is the hard part and the website does the persuading. For employment law, the search itself is the persuading: the person typing “wrongful termination lawyer near me” or “can I sue my employer for unpaid overtime” is deciding two things at once — do I have a claim, and can I afford to make one? The firms that win those searches are the ones whose pages answer both questions before asking for a phone call.

Here is how employment firms build search visibility that turns those searches into consultations — and consultations into signed cases.

1. Start with the claim, not the firm

Most employment law firm websites open with the firm: its history, its verdicts, its lawyers. A visitor who has just been fired skims past all of that, because none of it answers their question.

The first screen of a page that ranks — and converts — should answer three things within seconds:

  • Do you handle my situation? Name it plainly — wrongful termination, workplace discrimination, sexual harassment, unpaid wages and overtime, retaliation, denied leave, severance disputes — not “employment law” in general.
  • What does it cost to talk to you? Many visitors assume a lawyer is expensive, and many assume they cannot afford one at all. Say on the first screen that the consultation is free and confidential, and explain in plain language how employment fees usually work.
  • Will someone answer me soon? A visitor who is still employed may only have a short window to talk. A phone number, a messaging option, and a clear note about response time matter more than a biography.

Everything else — attorney biographies, office photos, credentials — belongs further down the page, after the visitor has decided the firm might be able to help. 2. Win the local searches first

Employment law is a local practice. Clients search with a city attached — “wrongful termination lawyer Chicago”, “employment attorney near me” — and they hire a firm they can meet. That makes local visibility the highest-return SEO work an employment firm can do.

  • Keep your Google Business Profile complete and honest. Accurate categories, services, hours, and a real office address. Employment clients are often choosing between two or three firms in the map pack; a complete, well-reviewed profile wins the click.
  • Earn reviews the right way. Ask every satisfied client, at the end of the matter, for an honest review — and reply to every review, good or bad, without revealing anything about a matter. Real reviews, shown in full, are the trust signal employment clients look for first.
  • Keep your name, address, and phone number consistent everywhere. Directories, bar listings, and your own website should all agree. Inconsistent details confuse search engines and cost you map-pack positions.
  • Build pages for the places you actually serve. One genuine page per city or county you practise in, with real local detail — the courts, the agencies, the commute. Resist the temptation to publish near-identical pages with only the city name changed: search engines treat thin, duplicated location pages harshly.

Our guide to ranking in the Google Map Pack covers the local visibility system in full; for employment firms, the map pack is where most signed cases begin, because the searcher is choosing a firm they can reach this week.

3. Build one page per claim type, not one page for employment law

“Employment lawyer” is a useful keyword, but it is also broad, competitive, and vague. A visitor searching for help with a specific problem — fired after filing a complaint, paid less than the minimum wage, harassed by a supervisor, denied overtime for years — wants a page that describes their situation, not a general page about employment law.

Build one clear, genuine page for each claim clients actually search for:

  • Wrongful termination — including firings after complaints, whistleblowing, and protected leave
  • Workplace discrimination — race, sex, age, disability, religion, and national origin
  • Sexual harassment and hostile work environments
  • Wage and hour claims — unpaid overtime, missed breaks, misclassification, and minimum wage
  • Retaliation — demotion, discipline, or dismissal after raising a concern
  • Severance agreements and non-compete disputes
  • Family and medical leave — denied leave, interference, and return-to-work problems
  • Disability accommodation and failure to accommodate

Each page should explain the claim in the visitor’s words, what the law generally says, what the process looks like, and what the first consultation covers — with the city and courts you serve named naturally. A page that genuinely answers the searcher’s question is the page that ranks, and it is also the page that earns the call.

That depth is also what separates a firm’s site from a directory listing. A directory can show a name and a phone number; only your own pages can show that you understand the visitor’s exact problem. 4. Answer the questions they ask before they call

An employment visitor arrives with the same questions almost every time: Was I wrongfully terminated? How much is my case worth? How long do I have to file a claim? What does a lawyer cost? Will my employer find out? Can I be fired for talking to a lawyer?

Your site should answer those questions honestly, in plain language, on the pages where they arise. That means:

  • Short, clear guides for each claim type — what the process looks like, what affects timing, what is usually needed
  • A genuine frequently-asked-questions section on each practice page, written from real intake questions, not from keyword tools alone
  • Clear statements about fees in general terms, and about what a consultation does and does not include
  • An honest note where the answer genuinely depends on the facts — whether a firing was unlawful depends on the reason, the evidence, and the jurisdiction, and saying so is more useful than a single promise

Keep the line between general information and legal advice visible: a page can explain how a wrongful termination claim usually works without telling a reader what will happen in their case. And never promise an outcome. “We will win your case” is a conclusion no lawyer can guarantee before hearing the facts. Say what you will do — listen, review the facts, explain the options honestly, and pursue the best result the law allows — and let the consultation carry the rest.

5. Explain fees and the contingency question honestly

Cost is the biggest barrier between an employment searcher and a consultation, and it is also the subject the fewest firm websites explain well. Most visitors have heard that employment lawyers work on contingency, but they do not know what that means for their case — and many assume that if they cannot pay up front, they cannot hire a lawyer at all.

Your pages should explain, in plain language:

  • What a free consultation covers. A review of what happened, an honest view of whether the facts point to a claim, and what the next steps would look like — with no obligation to hire the firm.
  • How fees are usually structured. Many employment matters are handled on a contingency basis, where the firm is paid from a recovery; others use hourly or flat-fee arrangements. Explain the general options without promising a particular arrangement for a case you have not reviewed.
  • What the client might still have to pay. Filing fees, agency charges, and other costs exist even in contingency matters. One honest paragraph about costs removes a real barrier to calling.
  • What happens if the case does not succeed. Visitors want to know the downside before they commit. An honest answer here builds more trust than any slogan.

Fee clarity is also an SEO advantage: “how much does an employment lawyer cost” and “do employment lawyers work on contingency” are searches with real intent, and a page that answers them well earns both the ranking and the consultation.

6. Earn trust without breaking the honesty rules

Employment clients arrive sceptical. They have seen the billboards and the television ads, and they are deciding whether to trust a stranger with a dispute that could affect their income, their reputation, and their next job. Your website has to prove the firm is real, experienced, and honest, without inventing anything.

  • Show real people. Real photos of the attorneys and staff, real names, a real office address, and a real phone number. Stock photos of handshakes and office towers tell a visitor nothing — and in this practice area, anonymity reads as a warning sign.
  • Describe experience in general terms. The types of claims handled, the agencies and courts served, and how the firm works a case are all honest, useful proof. Specific past results can only be shown where the firm’s jurisdiction allows it, and only with the figures the firm can actually support.
  • Handle reviews with care. Real reviews, shown in full, build trust. Never publish a client’s story, even anonymised, without proper consent, and never reply to a review in a way that reveals anything about a matter.
  • Explain the firm’s role honestly. Many visitors do not know what an employment lawyer actually does — reviewing the facts, preserving evidence, filing with the right agency, negotiating a settlement, and preparing for trial if needed. A short, plain explanation of that work is more convincing than any slogan.
  • Keep advertising rules visible. Every state has its own rules for attorney advertising. What the site says about results, fees, and specialisation has to comply with those rules, not just with marketing instinct.

7. Design for the still-employed searcher

Many employment clients are still working at the company they may have a claim against. They search in private — on a phone, on a lunch break, on a shared computer — and they worry about who might see the search. Your website should respect that.

  • No forced account creation or long forms. Every extra field is a reason to leave, and a reason to worry about who sees the information.
  • Say what happens to their information. One plain sentence — that anything they share in a consultation is confidential — matters more here than in most other practice areas.
  • Offer a discreet contact option. Some visitors cannot talk freely where they are, or do not want to explain what happened out loud yet. A messaging or email option, with a clear note about when you will reply, reaches people a phone call cannot.
  • Avoid aggressive retargeting. A visitor who sees your firm’s ads following them around the internet after searching about a dispute with their employer may feel watched rather than helped. Be careful with how your advertising follows people, and say so plainly in your privacy policy.
  • Keep the tone calm, not sensational. Fear-based headlines and “call now or lose everything” language repel the careful clients you want most. A steady, professional tone builds the trust that a signed case depends on.

Discretion is not a design detail in employment law. It is part of the service — and the website should show it from the first screen.

8. Intake: short, fast, and human

An employment visitor who has decided to reach out is often doing it in a narrow window — a lunch break, a commute, an evening after the children are asleep. The intake path has to respect that window, or the consultation goes to the next firm on the list.

  • Answer in minutes, not hours. Set a real response standard — for example, every inquiry answered within 15 minutes during business hours — and build the staffing to meet it. A standard you cannot keep is worse than no standard at all.
  • Keep the first form short. Name, phone or email, and one line about what happened. Asking for pay slips, contracts, or termination letters before the first conversation signals a firm that does not understand the visitor’s situation.
  • Show the next step, not a dead end. After the form is submitted, tell the visitor exactly what happens next: who will call, when, and what they will ask. A confirmation page that says nothing is a missed chance to reassure.
  • Follow up once, well. A visitor who does not answer the first call is not a lost client. One prompt, polite follow-up — a call and a short message — recovers clients that a single attempt would lose. More than that becomes pressure.
  • Protect the evidence early. In the consultation, explain what the client should keep — pay slips, emails, messages, performance reviews — and what they should not do, such as deleting messages or discussing the matter at work. Practical guidance in the first conversation is the service, not the sales pitch.

Speed also matters for the content itself: a visitor who is still employed will not read a long essay before calling. Put the phone number, the free consultation, and the first step at the top, and let the detailed explanation live further down for the visitors who want it.

9. Measure signed cases, not traffic

Employment marketing is easy to misjudge, because traffic is a poor proxy for clients. A blog post about a celebrity workplace dispute may draw visitors who will never hire you. A wrongful termination page with modest traffic that signs cases every week is worth far more.

Track, at minimum:

  • Calls and form submissions by page, so you know which claim pages earn their keep
  • Consultations booked — and consultations that become signed cases — by source and by claim type
  • Response time from first inquiry to first contact, by channel
  • Cost per signed case by channel, so you know what the website, the ads, and the map listing each actually deliver

Review those numbers monthly, not daily. Search visibility moves slowly, and a weekly panic about one ranking position leads to constant, unhelpful changes. A monthly review of signed cases per page tells you what to expand, what to rewrite, and what to stop publishing.

Where to start this month

You do not need to rebuild everything at once. For most small-to-midsize employment firms, this order returns the most signed cases for the least spend:

  • This week: make your phone number one tap away on every mobile page, shorten your contact form to the essentials, and put “free consultation” and the confidentiality note beside it
  • This month: rebuild your top three claim pages — wrongful termination, discrimination, and wage and hour — with plain-language explanations, the free-consultation offer, and a clear first step
  • This quarter: systematise review requests and fast responses, complete your Google Business Profile, and review signed cases per page before spending more on traffic

Employment clients are not looking for the loudest firm in their city. They are looking for the firm that understands what just happened to them at work, explains what it costs — nothing up front for the consultation — and answers when it matters. Be that firm in search, and your marketing will stop being a traffic exercise and start being a client pipeline.

Key Takeaways

  • An employment searcher is deciding two things at once: do I have a claim, and can I afford to make one? The page must answer both before asking for a call.
  • Local visibility is the highest-return SEO work for employment firms: a complete Google Business Profile, honest reviews, and genuine pages for the places you serve.
  • One genuine page per claim type — wrongful termination, discrimination, harassment, wage and hour, retaliation, severance, leave, accommodation — beats one general employment law page.
  • Answer the real intake questions on the page: was I wrongfully terminated, how much is my case worth, how long do I have, what does it cost, will my employer find out.
  • Fee clarity wins rankings and consultations: explain the free consultation, how fees usually work, and what costs may still apply.
  • Design for the still-employed searcher: short forms, a discreet contact option, a confidentiality note, and no aggressive retargeting.
  • Judge the work by signed cases per page and per claim type, not by traffic.
