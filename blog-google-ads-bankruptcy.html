Google Ads for Bankruptcy Lawyers: Turn Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 Searches Into Signed Clients

VL
Vertex Law MarketingPublished 26 November 2026 · 10 min read

A bankruptcy client rarely searches the way other legal clients search. They are not comparing firms on a quiet afternoon. They are searching in private, often late at night, under real financial stress — a foreclosure notice on the table, a car about to be repossessed, a phone that will not stop ringing. They type “bankruptcy lawyer near me” or “how much does bankruptcy cost” and they call the first firm that looks able to help without judging them.

That combination — urgency, privacy, and shame — is exactly why Google Ads works so well for bankruptcy firms, and exactly why it can waste money faster than most firms expect. Bankruptcy searches cover two very different kinds of case, a wide range of financial situations, and a large number of people who are researching rather than ready to file. A campaign that treats them all the same will pay for clicks from people who were never going to become clients. The firms that win are the ones whose campaigns are built for the way a bankruptcy client actually searches, at the moment they are ready to act, and in a tone that earns trust in seconds.

Here is how bankruptcy firms use Google Ads to turn Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 searches into signed clients — and how to stop paying for clicks that were never going to become cases.

1. Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 are different practices — build campaigns that way

The first mistake most bankruptcy firms make in Google Ads is running one campaign called “bankruptcy”. That single word covers two very different cases. A Chapter 7 case usually discharges most unsecured debts and is completed in a matter of months. A Chapter 13 case is a repayment plan that runs for three to five years. The clients are different, the fees are different, the timelines are different, and the value of a signed case is different. A campaign that lumps them together cannot bid correctly on either of them.

Split your campaigns by chapter first, and let each one have its own budget, its own ads, and its own landing pages. A Chapter 7 campaign should never share a budget with a Chapter 13 campaign: the Chapter 7 searcher usually wants a fast, clean break from debt, while the Chapter 13 searcher usually wants to keep a house or a car and pay back what they can over time. If they share one budget, the cheaper, higher-volume clicks will quietly consume the money that should have bought the higher-value cases.

Start with the case types that produce your best work, and build outward from there:

  • Chapter 7: liquidation bankruptcy for individuals. Searchers want to know whether they qualify, what it costs, what debts it clears, and how fast it ends the collection calls. High volume, and the decision often comes quickly once the fee and the process are clear.
  • Chapter 13: repayment-plan bankruptcy for people with regular income. Searchers are usually trying to stop a foreclosure or a repossession, or to catch up on debts they cannot clear in one go. Lower volume, higher case value, and a longer decision cycle.
  • Stopping foreclosure and repossession: searches driven by a deadline — a sale date, a repossession notice, a wage garnishment. These are the most urgent searches in bankruptcy law, and the ones where answering the phone first wins the case.
  • Debt relief and alternatives: searches from people who are not sure bankruptcy is right for them. Some become clients; many need an honest conversation first. Treat this as its own campaign with its own budget, not as a side effect of the Chapter 7 campaign.
  • Business bankruptcy: only if your firm genuinely handles it. Chapter 11 searches behave nothing like consumer bankruptcy searches, and they should never share a campaign with them.

2. Bid on the searches that can become clients — in the right order

Not every bankruptcy search is worth the same money. The highest-intent searches name the need and the lawyer in the same breath: “bankruptcy lawyer near me”, “chapter 7 attorney”, “chapter 13 lawyer”, “bankruptcy attorney” with a city attached. Those are the searches to win first, and to bid most aggressively on, because the searcher has already decided they need a lawyer and is choosing which one to call.

Below those, a second tier of searches is still valuable but should be bid on separately, at lower bids, with different ads: “how much does bankruptcy cost”, “chapter 7 vs chapter 13”, “can I keep my house if I file bankruptcy”, “how long does bankruptcy take”. These searchers are earlier in their decision. They may become clients — often after a consultation that answers the question honestly — but they convert at a lower rate, and they should not share a budget with the searches that are ready to call now.

Keep the two tiers in separate campaigns with separate budgets. And remember that paid search does not work alone: our guide to ranking in the Google Map Pack covers the local visibility system, and the firms that win both channels own the first screen for bankruptcy searches in their city.

  • Highest intent: bankruptcy, Chapter 7, or Chapter 13 + lawyer or attorney + city, and “near me” versions of the same. Bid here first.
  • High intent: specific situations — “stop foreclosure lawyer”, “chapter 13 attorney near me”, “file chapter 7”, “bankruptcy lawyer free consultation”. Separate ad groups, matched landing pages.
  • Research intent: costs, eligibility, the means test, what bankruptcy clears, whether a house or car can be kept, “do I need a lawyer to file bankruptcy”. Lower bids, helpful landing pages, and a clear path to a consultation.
  • Not worth bidding on: free bankruptcy help, legal aid, pro bono, do-it-yourself filing guides, bankruptcy forms and instructions, and news about companies filing for bankruptcy. These belong on your negative keyword list, not in your campaigns.

3. Speak to a searcher under financial stress — and mean it

This is the single biggest difference between bankruptcy Google Ads and every other practice area, and the one most firms miss entirely. A bankruptcy searcher is usually embarrassed, anxious, and afraid of being judged. They have often put off calling a lawyer for months because they felt ashamed. The ad they click is the first thing that either confirms that fear or relieves it.

Write ads that sound like a steady, respectful professional, not a salesperson. Say the things that actually reassure someone in this position: the consultation is free and confidential, the first call is a conversation rather than a commitment, and the firm has helped people in this exact situation before. Never use shame, fear, or pressure in the copy, and never promise a specific outcome — advertising rules in many states limit what a firm can promise about results, and a promise you cannot keep is the fastest way to lose the careful clients you want most.

  • Lead with confidentiality and a free consultation. “Free, confidential consultation” is the line that gets a hesitant searcher to call. It removes the two fears that stop the call: that it will cost money, and that someone will find out.
  • Name the relief, honestly. Filing usually brings an automatic stay that stops collection calls, lawsuits, foreclosure, and repossession while the case is active. Say that plainly, without exaggeration, and let the consultation explain how it applies to their situation.
  • Say what the first call covers. A review of the debts, the income, and the assets; an honest answer about whether bankruptcy is the right step; and a clear explanation of the cost and the payment options. People call when they know what the call will be like.
  • Mention payment plans where you offer them. Cost is the main reason a bankruptcy searcher does not call. If the firm offers payment plans, say so in the ad. A searcher who believes they cannot afford a lawyer will not click an ad that says nothing about money.
  • Keep the tone calm and human. No exclamation marks, no countdown language, no warnings about what will happen if they wait. A person in financial trouble responds to steadiness, not to alarm.

Tone is not decoration in bankruptcy advertising. It is the targeting decision that decides whether the right searcher clicks your ad or the next firm’s.

4. Match the ad to the chapter, not to the firm

Most bankruptcy ads read the same: the firm name, “free consultation”, a phone number, and a line about experience. A searcher choosing between Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 skims past that, because it does not tell them whether this firm handles their kind of case.

Build one ad group per case type — Chapter 7, Chapter 13, stopping foreclosure, debt relief alternatives — and write the ads for that need alone. A Chapter 7 ad should say Chapter 7, name the free consultation, and say what the first call covers. A Chapter 13 ad should say Chapter 13, because a searcher trying to save a house is looking for a firm that understands their situation specifically.

  • Use the searcher’s words in the headline. If the search was “chapter 7 lawyer”, the headline should say Chapter 7 lawyer, not “debt help available”.
  • Answer the cost question in the ad. A line about a free consultation and payment plans does more work than any claim about experience, because cost is the question every bankruptcy searcher is already asking.
  • Use call assets and location assets. On mobile, most bankruptcy clicks should become phone calls, not website visits. Make the phone number one tap away, and show the office location so the searcher knows the firm is local.
  • Test call-only ads for the highest-intent searches. When someone searches “bankruptcy lawyer near me” at 9 p.m. from a phone, the fastest path to a signed client is a call, not a landing page. Call-only ads remove the step in between.
  • Keep Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 ads apart. A searcher who needs a repayment plan and clicks an ad that talks about wiping out debt will feel misled, and a searcher who wants a clean break and lands on a page about five-year plans will leave. The wrong match wastes the click twice over.

The same rule applies to the page the ad leads to. An ad about stopping foreclosure that lands on a general bankruptcy homepage is a broken promise, and a searcher under pressure will not wait to work out where the foreclosure information lives.

5. Build landing pages that answer the money questions first

A bankruptcy click is bought from a visitor who is worried about money above all else, and who is deciding between you and two other firms in other tabs. The landing page has to earn the click immediately, because the visitor will not scroll through attorney biographies to find the answer to the one question they actually have: what will this cost me, and what happens if I call.

The first screen should answer three things before anything else: do you handle my kind of case, what does it cost to talk to you, and what happens when I call. A phone number that is one tap away, a short form — name, phone, and one line about the situation — and a plain statement that the consultation is free and confidential. Everything else — attorney biographies, office photos, practice descriptions — belongs further down, after the visitor has decided the firm might be able to help.

  • One page per case type. The Chapter 7 page talks about Chapter 7: who qualifies, what it clears, how long it takes, and what it costs. The Chapter 13 page does the same for Chapter 13. A page that genuinely answers the searcher’s question is the page that converts — and it is also the page Google rewards with a better quality score, which lowers what you pay per click.
  • Be honest about cost. Bankruptcy clients expect a straight answer about fees, and many firms lose them by hiding it. Explain that fees vary by case, give the factors that decide them, and say clearly that payment plans are available where they are. A visitor who cannot find any mention of money assumes the worst and leaves.
  • Answer the keep-or-lose questions. Can I keep my house? My car? My retirement savings? These are the questions every bankruptcy searcher is asking, and a page that answers them in plain language — noting that exemptions vary by state — is the page that earns the call.
  • Load fast on a phone. A slow page on a mobile connection loses the click you just paid for. Keep images light, remove anything that delays the phone number and the form, and test the page on a real phone, not only on a desktop.
  • Show real people. Real photos of the attorneys and staff, a real office address, and a real phone number. Stock photos of handshakes tell a worried visitor nothing — and in this practice area, anonymity reads as a warning sign.
  • Do not ask for what you do not need yet. Account numbers, creditor lists, and detailed financial records come after the first conversation, not before it. A form that asks for them up front signals a firm that does not understand the visitor’s situation.
  • Confirm what happens next. After the form is submitted, say exactly what happens: who will call, when, and what they will ask. A confirmation page that says nothing is a missed chance to reassure someone who is already anxious.

6. Cut the wasted spend with negative keywords

In bankruptcy, the difference between a profitable campaign and a money pit is often the negative keyword list. Because the searches are so broad — from company bankruptcies in the news to do-it-yourself filing guides to free legal help — an unfocused campaign will pay for a great many clicks that could never become clients.

Start with the obvious exclusions and review your search terms report every week to find more:

  • Jobs and education: jobs, hiring, salary, law school, paralegal, internship, “how to become a bankruptcy lawyer”
  • Free and DIY: free bankruptcy help, pro bono, legal aid, “file bankruptcy myself”, bankruptcy forms, “how to file bankruptcy without a lawyer”, form downloads and instructions
  • News and companies: company names in the news, “company files for bankruptcy”, Chapter 11 for large corporations — unless your firm genuinely handles business cases, in which case this is a separate campaign with its own budget
  • Wrong places: cities and states you do not serve, unless the searcher is in your market
  • Wrong case types: if you do not handle business bankruptcy, exclude Chapter 11 terms; if you do not take debt settlement cases, exclude those terms rather than paying for their clicks
  • Unrelated debts: student loan forgiveness programmes, tax debt settlement, credit repair, debt consolidation loans — searches that sound close to bankruptcy but are not your cases

The search terms report is where this work happens. Every week, look at what people actually typed before they clicked your ad, and add every term that could not have become a client to the negative list. Over a month, this single habit can cut a meaningful share of wasted spend — and the savings go straight into the searches that do become clients.

7. Win the call: intake speed and a payment plan conversation

Bankruptcy clients call the firm that answers — and the firm that makes the money conversation easy. A searcher in financial trouble will often call two or three firms in a row; the one that picks up first, listens without judgement, and explains the cost and the payment options plainly usually gets the case. That makes intake — not the ad copy, not the bid — the single biggest lever on your cost per signed client.

  • Answer in minutes, including evenings. Bankruptcy searches spike in the evening, when people are home, alone, and finally free to deal with the problem. A call that goes to voicemail at 8 p.m. is a client for the next firm on the list.
  • Keep the first call short and human. The caller needs to hear that the firm handles their kind of case, what the consultation costs, and what the next step is. A script that asks for financial details before it offers reassurance loses callers.
  • Talk about payment plans early. Most bankruptcy clients cannot pay a full fee up front — that is why they are calling. A firm that explains its payment options on the first call signs clients a firm that waits until the consultation never meets.
  • Follow up once, well. A caller who does not answer the first callback is not a lost client. One prompt, polite follow-up — a call and a short message — recovers cases a single attempt would lose. More than that becomes pressure, and a person under financial stress does not need more pressure.
  • Track the call, not just the click. Call tracking numbers per campaign, and a note in your intake system of which campaign and keyword produced each call, turn Google Ads from a traffic bill into a client pipeline you can judge.

This is also where many firms discover their ads were working all along and their intake was not. Before raising the budget, check how many calls went unanswered, how long callbacks took, and how many consultations never got booked. Fixing intake is usually cheaper than buying more clicks.

8. Target the geography and the hours where clients search

Bankruptcy is a local practice for most firms, and your campaigns should be built that way. A firm in one city should not be paying for clicks in another state, and a firm that serves a whole metro area should know which parts of it produce its clients.

  • Target where you actually practise. Set location targeting to the cities and counties you serve, and use “people in your targeted locations” rather than “people searching about your locations”, so a searcher in another state looking up your city does not cost you a click.
  • Watch which areas sign clients. When the data shows one part of your market signs clients at a lower cost than another, shift budget toward it. Over a quarter, that reallocation alone can move your cost per signed client noticeably.
  • Schedule ads for when people search — and when you can answer. Bankruptcy searches spike in the evening and on weekends, when people are free to research and call. But if no one can answer the phone at 10 p.m., paying for those clicks wastes them. Either staff the hours or schedule the ads around the hours you can genuinely serve, and use call scheduling so the phone number only shows when someone is there to pick up.
  • Separate mobile from desktop. Most bankruptcy searches happen on a phone, and mobile clicks convert differently — usually into calls, at a different rate and cost. Keep mobile and desktop performance visible separately so you are not judging a call-heavy mobile campaign by desktop form-fill standards.

9. Budget by signed client, not by click

The question most bankruptcy firms ask first — how much should we spend on Google Ads — is the wrong question until you know what a signed client is worth to the firm and what you are willing to pay to sign one. Our guide to how much a law firm should spend on Google Ads gives the budgeting framework in full; for bankruptcy, three rules matter most.

  • Work back from the client, not forward from the click. Decide what a signed client is worth and what you can afford to pay to sign one, then judge the campaign by cost per signed client. A campaign with expensive clicks and a low cost per signed client is a good campaign; a campaign with cheap clicks and no signed clients is a waste of money.
  • Remember that Chapter 13 is worth more than Chapter 7. A Chapter 13 case runs for years and is worth substantially more in fees than a Chapter 7 case, so a higher cost per signed Chapter 13 client can still be the better deal. Judge the two chapters separately, or the Chapter 7 numbers will make the Chapter 13 campaign look worse than it is.
  • Give a new campaign enough budget to learn. A budget so small that it buys a handful of clicks a day will never produce enough data to judge, and pausing it after a week tells you nothing. Fund it properly for a full month, with the structure in place, before deciding whether it works.
  • Reallocate, do not just increase. When one chapter signs clients at a lower cost than another, move budget toward it. When one keyword produces calls that never sign, pause it and give the money to the keywords that do. Most bankruptcy campaigns improve more from reallocation than from a bigger total budget.

Judge the spend monthly, not daily. Click costs move with competition, and a single expensive day means nothing. A month of cost per consultation and cost per signed client, by campaign and by chapter, tells you what to expand, what to fix, and what to stop.

10. Measure signed clients, not clicks

Google Ads reports clicks, impressions, and click-through rates by default. None of those is a client. For a bankruptcy firm, the numbers that matter are consultations booked, consultations that become signed clients, and cost per signed client — by campaign, by chapter, and by keyword where the data allows.

  • Connect the ad to the signed client. Use call tracking, form tracking, and your intake or case management system together, so a signed client can be traced back to the campaign that produced it. Without that link, you are guessing which half of the budget works.
  • Import offline conversions. A click becomes a consultation, and a consultation becomes a signed client, days or weeks later. Feeding signed clients back into Google Ads as offline conversions lets the system bid toward the searches that actually produce clients, not just the ones that produce clicks.
  • Review search terms weekly, performance monthly. The search terms report catches wasted spend early; the monthly review of cost per signed client is where the real decisions happen — which chapters to grow, which keywords to pause, and where the next dollar goes.
  • Keep the rest of the funnel honest. Ads bring the call, but the website, the intake, and the follow-up sign the client. Where to start this month

You do not need to rebuild everything at once. For most small-to-midsize bankruptcy firms, this order returns the most signed clients for the least spend:

  • This week: split your highest-intent keywords into their own campaigns by chapter, add the obvious negative keywords, and check how many calls went unanswered last week — and at which hours
  • This month: rebuild your top two landing pages — Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 — with one-tap calling, a short form, honest cost information, and a clear statement of what happens next; start reviewing the search terms report weekly
  • This quarter: connect signed clients back to campaigns with call tracking and offline conversion imports, then reallocate budget toward the chapter with the lowest cost per signed client

Bankruptcy clients are not looking for the loudest firm in their city. They are looking for the firm that answers without judgement, explains what it costs and what happens next, and follows up when it matters. Be that firm in the ad, on the page, and on the phone, and Google Ads stops being the most uncertain line in your marketing budget and starts being the most reliable source of signed clients you have.

Key Takeaways

  • Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 are different practices: split campaigns by chapter — each with its own budget, ads, and landing pages — because the clients, fees, timelines, and case values are different.
  • Bid hardest on bankruptcy, Chapter 7, or Chapter 13 + lawyer + city searches, lower on research questions, and never let the two share one budget.
  • Speak to a searcher under financial stress: lead with a free, confidential consultation, name the relief honestly, and say plainly what the first call covers.
  • One ad group per case type, with ads and landing pages written for that need alone; a Chapter 13 ad should never land on a general bankruptcy homepage.
  • Answer the money questions first on the landing page — cost, payment plans, and whether the house and car can be kept — and ask only for what you need to start the conversation.
  • Negative keywords and a weekly search terms review are the fastest way to cut wasted spend in bankruptcy campaigns.
  • Intake speed and an early payment plan conversation are the real conversion rate: the firm that answers first and makes the money talk easy usually signs the client.
  • Target the areas you serve, schedule ads around the hours you can answer, and judge mobile and desktop performance separately.
  • Budget by cost per signed client, not cost per click — judge Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 separately, and feed signed clients back into Google Ads as offline conversions so the system bids toward real clients.
