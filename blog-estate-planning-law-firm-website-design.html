Estate Planning Law Firm Website Design: How to Turn Will, Trust, and Probate Searches Into Consultations

VL
Vertex Law MarketingPublished 24 November 2026 · 10 min read

An estate planning client is not in a hurry the way a DUI client is. They are a new parent who finally wants a will, an adult child helping an aging parent put affairs in order, a homeowner who wants the house protected for the kids, or a family that just lost someone and now faces probate. They are not panicking — they are thinking. They read carefully, they compare firms, and they often take days or weeks before they call anyone.

That slower, more deliberate decision is exactly why most estate planning websites fail. They are built like brochure sites: a stock photo of a handshake, a list of services, a contact form, and nothing that answers the questions the visitor is actually weighing. The firms that sign estate planning clients online are the ones whose websites respect how these clients decide — calmly, with clear information, honest answers about cost, and a first step that feels easy rather than intimidating.

Here is how estate planning firms design websites that turn will, trust, and probate searches into consultations.

1. Lead with the life moment, not the firm’s credentials

Most estate planning websites open with the firm: years in practice, bar admissions, a mission statement. A visitor who just had a baby, or whose mother was just diagnosed with dementia, does not care about any of that yet. They care about their own situation — and whether this firm understands it.

Lead with the visitor’s life moment, in their words. The hero section should name the situations you help with — a first will after a new baby, a trust to protect a family home, probate after losing a parent, a power of attorney for an aging parent, guardianship for minor children — and say plainly that the first consultation is free and that the firm will explain the options in plain language, without pressure. A headline like “Protect your family’s future — a clear plan, explained in plain English” does more work than any list of credentials, because it tells the visitor they are in the right place and that the process will not be intimidating.

The firm’s experience still matters, but it belongs further down the page, after the visitor has decided the site understands their situation. Trust in estate planning is built in this order: you understand what I am going through, you explain things clearly, and only then, you are qualified to help.

2. Design for the readers who actually hire you — including older adults

Estate planning clients skew older than most practice areas. Many are in their sixties, seventies, and eighties, and many are adult children researching on a parent’s behalf. A website with small gray text, low contrast, tiny buttons, and dense paragraphs will lose them — not because they are uninterested, but because they literally cannot read it comfortably.

Make the type large and the contrast strong. Body text should be easy to read without zooming, links should look like links, and buttons should be big enough to tap with confidence. Avoid light gray text on white backgrounds, decorative fonts for body copy, and anything that moves or flashes while the visitor is trying to read. A calm, uncluttered page with generous spacing feels trustworthy to an estate planning visitor; a busy, flashy page feels like a sales pitch.

Test the site the way your clients will use it: on a phone, in bright light, with the browser’s text size turned up. If the site is hard to read in those conditions, it is hard to read for a large share of the people who would hire you. Speed matters here too — a page that loads in a couple of seconds keeps the visitor, and a heavy page full of oversized images and autoplay video does not.

3. Build one page per service, not one page for estate planning

A single “Estate Planning” page that lists wills, trusts, probate, and powers of attorney in a few sentences each serves nobody: it is too thin to rank, too vague to reassure, and too generic to convert. Estate planning firms that win online give each service its own page.

Each page should be written for the person who needs that service. A wills page should explain what a will does and does not do, what happens without one, and what the process of making one involves. A trusts page should explain the difference between a revocable living trust and a testamentary trust in plain language, and when a trust is worth the extra cost. A probate page should explain what probate is, how long it usually takes, what it costs, and what the family’s responsibilities are. A powers of attorney page should explain the difference between financial and health care authority, and why having both matters before a crisis.

  • Wills — what a will covers, what happens without one, and how the process works
  • Revocable living trusts — when a trust is worth it, and what it costs to set up
  • Probate and estate administration — what families face after a death, and how the firm helps
  • Powers of attorney — financial and health care authority, before a crisis makes it urgent
  • Health care directives and living wills — making medical wishes clear in writing
  • Guardianship for minor children — naming who raises your children if something happens to you
  • Elder law and Medicaid planning — protecting assets while planning for long-term care

Each page should name the city and the courts you serve naturally, explain the process in the visitor’s words, and end with the same clear next step: a free, confidential consultation. One strong page per service, with genuine local detail, beats a dozen thin pages — and search engines treat near-identical pages with only the city name changed harshly. 4. Answer the cost question honestly

Cost is the question almost every estate planning visitor is afraid to ask — and the one most estate planning websites refuse to answer. Many people put off making a will for years because they assume a lawyer will cost far more than it does. A website that says nothing about fees leaves them to assume the worst, and they leave.

You do not need to publish a price list, and in most cases you should not. But you can say a great deal without one. Explain how fees generally work: whether simple wills are handled on a flat fee, what a typical estate plan includes, whether the first consultation is free, and that the firm will give a clear estimate before any work begins. A page that says “most of our straightforward wills are handled on a flat fee; the first consultation is free, and you will know the full cost before you commit to anything” removes the barrier that stops many people from calling.

Be just as honest about probate costs. Families who have just lost someone are worried about what the process will cost and how long it will take. A page that explains the fee structures used in your state — flat fee, hourly, or a percentage of the estate, where the law allows — and gives a realistic sense of the timeline will earn far more trust than a page that says “call for a quote” and nothing else.

5. Explain the process before you ask for the call

Estate planning clients delay because they do not know what the process involves. They imagine long meetings, piles of paperwork, and legal language they will not understand. A website that explains the process step by step removes that fear.

Show the visitor exactly what happens. A simple outline works: first, a free consultation where the attorney listens and explains the options; second, a draft of the documents for the client to review at home; third, a signing meeting where everything is executed properly; and finally, a clear explanation of where the documents are kept and what to do when life changes. A short paragraph for each step, in plain language, tells the visitor that the process is manageable — and that the firm will guide them through it.

The same applies to probate. Families who have just lost someone do not know what probate requires of them. A page that explains the first steps — what documents to gather, what the court filing involves, what the executor’s responsibilities are — is genuinely helpful, and it is the page a grieving family will remember when they choose a firm.

6. Speak to the family, not just the individual

Estate planning decisions are rarely made alone. A will is often discussed with a spouse. A trust is often set up after a conversation with adult children. A power of attorney is often researched by the child who will be named in it. Your website should speak to all of them.

That means writing for the adult child as well as the parent. A page that says “if you are helping a parent put their affairs in order, here is what to expect, and here is how the first meeting works” speaks directly to a large share of your real visitors. It also means acknowledging the emotions involved: these are decisions about death, incapacity, and family conflict, and a site that treats them with warmth and plain language will be trusted far more than one that treats them as transactions.

Consider a simple downloadable checklist — “what to bring to your first estate planning meeting” or “five documents every family should have” — offered in exchange for an email address. It is genuinely useful, it gives the visitor a reason to stay in touch, and it starts the relationship before the consultation. Keep the ask small: a name and an email address, nothing more.

7. Prove trust honestly — real people, real process

Estate planning is a trust business. A visitor is choosing the person who will write the documents that decide what happens to their family, their home, and their children. They need to believe the lawyer is competent, careful, and human. A website proves that with real people and a real process, not with invented results or borrowed authority.

Attorney pages should feel like people, not résumés. A real photo, a plain-language explanation of what the attorney focuses on, and a short note on how they approach estate planning — listening first, explaining in plain English, and making sure the documents match the family’s actual wishes — tells a visitor far more than a list of bar admissions. If an attorney has genuine training that matters to clients, such as an LL.M. in taxation or estate planning certification, say so. If not, do not invent it.

The same honesty rule applies to reviews and results. Genuine client reviews, shown with the client’s own words and attributed properly, are powerful. Fabricated testimonials, invented statistics, and outcome guarantees are not — they mislead visitors, they breach advertising rules in many states, and they destroy trust the moment a client discovers the gap. If the firm is new and has few reviews, that is fine: explain the process, show the people, and let the consultation do the convincing. 8. A consultation form that feels easy, not intrusive

The consultation form is where an estate planning website wins or loses the client, and most firms make it far too hard. A visitor who is thinking about a will does not want to fill in a long form asking about their assets, their children’s names, and their medical history before they have even spoken to a lawyer. A form that demands all of that will be abandoned halfway through.

Ask for three things at the first step and nothing else: a name, a way to reach them, and what they need help with — a short, free-text box where they can write “need a will, two young kids, just bought a house” in their own words. That is enough for the firm to call back with useful help. Everything else — the details of the estate, the family situation, the existing documents — belongs in the consultation itself, not in a form.

Make the form work on a phone with a thumb, label every field clearly, and confirm the submission in plain language: “Thank you — we have received your message and will call you back within one business day.” Better still, say what happens next: who will call, when, and what the visitor should have ready. And test the form regularly — a form that silently fails to send is a hole in the bottom of the firm’s pipeline that nobody notices until the month’s numbers come in.

9. Local pages that actually help

Estate planning is a local business: clients want a lawyer who knows their state’s laws, their county’s probate court, and their community. The website should show that local knowledge, but with genuine detail rather than duplicated pages.

A location or “serving” page for each city or county the firm covers should include real local information: which court handles probate in that county, how the probate process generally works there, and any state-specific rules that affect the documents — for example, whether the state has an estate tax, how the homestead rules work, or what the state’s default rules are when someone dies without a will. That is the kind of detail a visitor recognizes as genuine, and it is the kind of detail search engines reward.

What does not work is publishing near-identical pages for twenty towns with only the town name changed. Thin, duplicated location pages do not rank, and they make the firm look like a directory rather than a local practice. One honest, detailed page per place you genuinely serve beats a wall of copy-and-paste. 10. Measure consultations, not traffic

An estate planning website can get more traffic every month and still sign fewer clients, if the traffic is the wrong kind or the site does not convert it. The number to watch is not visits — it is consultations booked, and consultations that become signed estate plans.

Track the basics properly: how many consultation requests the site produces each month, which pages those requests come from, how many become signed clients, and how long it takes the firm to respond. A wills page that brings in ten consultations a month is worth more than a blog post that brings in a thousand visitors and no calls. Estate planning clients often take weeks to decide, so track them over a longer window than a DUI or personal injury firm would — a visitor who reads the site in November may call in January, and that is still a win the website produced.

That measurement is also what makes the next round of improvements obvious. If the trusts page converts and the probate page does not, the probate page is the one to rewrite. If mobile visitors request consultations at half the rate of desktop visitors, the mobile experience is the problem. A website is not finished when it launches; it is finished when the numbers say it is working. Where to start this month

If the firm’s site needs work, do not try to rebuild everything at once. Start with the changes that matter most to an estate planning visitor.

  • This week: make the type larger and the contrast stronger, cut the consultation form down to a name, a contact, and a short message, and check the site on a phone with the text size turned up.
  • This month: give wills, trusts, and probate each their own page with honest answers to the questions clients actually ask, and add a plain-language explanation of fees and the first meeting.
  • This quarter: add genuine local pages for the cities and counties you serve, add a simple checklist download for visitors who are not ready to call yet, and start tracking consultations per page over a longer decision window.

Estate planning clients choose the firm that explains clearly, earns trust slowly, and makes the first step easy. A website that leads with their life moment, answers the cost question honestly, explains the process in plain language, and makes the consultation effortless will win those clients — and keep winning them, month after month.
