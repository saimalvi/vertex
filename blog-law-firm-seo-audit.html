Law Firm SEO Audit: How to Find What's Holding Your Firm Back on Google

VL
Vertex Law MarketingPublished 25 November 2026 · 10 min read

Most law firms that call us about SEO do not actually have an SEO problem they can name. They have a symptom: the phone is quiet, the map pack shows competitors, or the website ranks for the wrong searches. The instinct is to buy more of everything — more pages, more blog posts, more links — and hope something moves. That is how firms spend thousands of dollars and end up in the same position six months later.

An audit does the opposite. Before a dollar is spent on new work, it finds out exactly what is holding the firm back: the pages Google cannot see, the searches the site does not match, the local profile that is half-finished, the intake path that loses callers. Most firms are surprised by how much of the problem is fixable without a single new blog post.

Here is how to run that audit on your own firm, in the order that matters.

1. Start with what Google actually sees

The first question is not how high you rank. It is whether Google can find and understand your pages at all. A page that is not indexed cannot rank, and a page that is indexed badly ranks for the wrong thing.

Check the basics first:

  • Search for your firm by name. Your homepage and your main practice-area pages should appear. If a core page does not show up, something is wrong — it may be blocked by a setting, buried under a redirect, or missing from the sitemap.
  • Search for a page’s exact title. If the page exists on your site but does not appear for its own title, Google may have chosen a different page as the better match, or it may not be indexing the page at all.
  • Check for duplicate pages. Many firms end up with two or three versions of the same service page — one from an old redesign, one with a slightly different URL, one in a city-specific folder. Google splits the ranking strength between them, and none of them ranks well.
  • Look for thin pages. A practice-area page with two short paragraphs and a contact form rarely outranks a competitor’s page that explains the service, the process, the costs, and the first step. Thin pages are the most common reason a firm ranks on page two instead of page one.

None of this needs a tool to spot. A search engine results check and a careful look at the site usually surface the big problems in under an hour. The point of this step is simple: fix the foundation before building anything on top of it.

2. Check whether your pages match what clients actually search

The most common audit finding is a mismatch between the firm’s pages and the client’s language. The firm writes about “matrimonial dissolution proceedings.” The client searches for “divorce lawyer near me.” The firm writes about “premises liability.” The client searches for “slip and fall lawyer.”

Go through your main service pages and ask, for each one:

  • Does the page use the words a client would type into Google? The plain phrase belongs in the page title, the first heading, and the opening paragraph — not buried three sections down.
  • Does the page answer the question behind the search? A visitor searching for a DUI lawyer after an arrest wants to know what happens next, what it costs, and whether the firm handles first offenses. A page that only lists the firm’s credentials does not answer any of that.
  • Is there one page per service, or one page trying to cover everything? A single “practice areas” page cannot rank for divorce, custody, and child support at the same time. Each service clients search for deserves its own page, written in the client’s words.

Our guide to family law SEO shows how this works in one practice area: the firms that win are the ones whose pages speak the client’s language for divorce, custody, and support separately, instead of lumping them together. The same principle applies to every practice area, from personal injury to estate planning.

3. Audit your local visibility — the profile, the reviews, and the map pack

For most firms, the local results matter more than the organic rankings. A client searching for a lawyer in their city looks at the map pack first, and the firms there get the first calls. If your firm is invisible in local search, the rest of the audit barely matters.

Work through the local checklist:

  • Google Business Profile. Is the name, address, and phone number correct and identical to the website? Are the right categories selected — not just “lawyer,” but the specific practice area? Are there real photos of the office and the team, and hours that match the site? A half-finished profile is the single most common local SEO failure we see.
  • Reviews. How many do you have, how recent are they, and do they mention the service the client hired you for? A review that says “helped us after the car accident” tells the next visitor far more than “great lawyer.” A firm with three reviews from two years ago loses to a firm with forty recent ones, even if the older firm is better.
  • Consistency everywhere else. Directories, bar association listings, and local business listings should all carry the same name, address, and phone number. Small mismatches add up, and Google treats them as a reason to trust the listing less.
  • The map pack itself. Search for your main service plus your city from a phone, in the area you serve. If competitors appear and you do not, note who is there and what their profiles have that yours does not — usually more reviews, more photos, or a more complete profile.

Our guide to ranking in the Google Map Pack covers this in detail, including the three factors Google uses to rank local firms and the review process that actually produces results.

4. Test the site the way a client experiences it

A firm can rank well and still lose the client, because the site fails the moment a visitor arrives. Most clients reach a law firm website on a phone, often in a stressful moment, and they decide within seconds whether to call or go back to the results.

Run through the site as a client would:

  • Speed. Does the homepage load in a few seconds on a phone connection? Large images, video backgrounds, and chat widgets that load before the content all slow a site down, and slow sites lose visitors before they read a word.
  • Mobile. Is the phone number tappable? Is the contact form usable on a small screen without pinching and zooming? Is the text readable without horizontal scrolling? More than half of law firm searches happen on a phone, and a site that fights the visitor on mobile is a site that loses cases.
  • Trust signals. Does the site show real attorneys, a real office, and a real address? Stock photos of gavels and courthouses do not build trust. A visitor who cannot tell who they would be calling is a visitor who calls someone else.
  • The first screen. Does the top of the homepage say, in plain words, what the firm does, where it does it, and what the visitor should do next? If the first screen is a slogan and a photo, the visitor has to work to find the answer — and most will not.

Our guide to website design that converts covers the structure a law firm homepage needs, from the first screen to the intake form. An audit that ignores the visitor’s experience misses the part of SEO that actually produces clients.

5. Check the intake path — where the consultation dies

This is the step most audits skip, and it is often where the money is. Rankings bring visitors. The intake path turns visitors into consultations. If the path is broken, better rankings just bring more people to a broken door.

Walk the path yourself:

  • Fill in the contact form. Does it ask for too much? A form with twelve fields gets fewer submissions than one with four. Name, phone number, a short description of the situation, and a preferred time to call is enough for a first contact.
  • Call the number on the site. Does someone answer, and how fast? A firm that misses calls during business hours is paying for visibility it cannot use.
  • Check what happens after hours. Most urgent legal searches happen outside business hours — evenings, weekends, after an arrest or an accident. A firm with no after-hours plan — a call service, a voicemail that promises a fast callback, a form that says when to expect a reply — loses those callers to the firm that answers.
  • Time the response. If a visitor submits a form on Monday and hears back on Wednesday, they have already hired someone else. The firms that win the most work from search respond fastest, often within the hour.

6. Look at the backlink profile — quality over volume

Links from other websites are still one of the strongest ranking signals, but the audit question is not how many links you have. It is where they come from.

A healthy profile for a law firm looks like this: links from local news, local business directories, bar associations, community organizations, and other local businesses. A suspicious profile looks like this: hundreds of links from unrelated blogs, directories nobody has heard of, or sites in other countries that have nothing to do with law.

Check for three problems:

  • Too few real links. A firm with almost no links from local or legal sources is easy for competitors to outrank, because Google has little outside confirmation that the firm is real and established.
  • Low-quality links. Links bought from link farms or built by an old SEO company can hurt more than they help. If a previous agency built links you cannot explain, that is worth investigating before it becomes a problem.
  • Lost links. Links from a local news story, a sponsorship, or a directory that changed its site can disappear quietly. A firm that earned good links and lost them has already done the hard work once — recovering them is often the fastest win in the whole audit.

7. Compare against the firms outranking you

An audit that only looks inward misses the point. Rankings are relative: you do not need a perfect site, you need a better one than the firms above you. Pick the three firms that rank ahead of you for your most valuable search — the one that brings the cases you want — and compare honestly:

  • How many pages do they have for that practice area, and how much better are they? One strong page can beat ten thin ones, but it rarely beats five strong ones.
  • How many reviews do they have, and how recent? If the gap is twenty reviews, that is a fixable problem with a real process. If the gap is two hundred, the strategy has to be different.
  • What does their site do that yours does not? A clear fee explanation, an honest FAQ, a page that names the local courts and what happens there — the details that make a visitor feel the firm understands their situation are usually visible within a minute of reading.
  • Where are they weak? Every competitor has a gap: a practice area they barely cover, a city they do not serve well, a page that has not been updated in years. That gap is the opening.

The goal is not to copy the competitor. It is to understand what the standard is in your market, and where you can realistically beat it first.

8. Turn the audit into a priority list

An audit that ends as a long list of problems is not useful. What matters is the order. Fix in this sequence, and stop when the next item costs more than it returns:

First, the blockers. Pages Google cannot see, a broken contact form, a wrong phone number, a suspended or unverified Business Profile. These cost clients every day they stay broken, and they are usually cheap to fix.

Second, the local foundation. A complete profile, a review process, consistent listings. For most firms, this is where the fastest ranking improvement comes from, because local results move faster than organic ones.

Third, the pages that matter most. One strong page for your most valuable service, written in the client’s words, beats ten new blog posts. Start with the service that brings the best cases, then work down the list.

Fourth, the intake path. Faster responses, a shorter form, an after-hours plan. This turns the traffic you already have into more consultations, which is the cheapest growth a firm can buy.

Last, the long work. Links, content, and authority build slowly. They matter, but they should not come before the fixes that produce clients this month.

How often should a firm audit its SEO?

Once a year at minimum, and every time something changes: a redesign, a new office, a new practice area, a sudden drop in calls, or a competitor that appears out of nowhere in the map pack. An audit after a redesign is especially important, because redesigns are the most common cause of sudden ranking losses — pages disappear, URLs change, content is cut, and nobody notices until the phone goes quiet.

The audit itself does not have to be expensive or complicated. The checks above can be done in a day with a search engine, a phone, and an honest look at the site. What takes expertise is not finding the problems — it is knowing which ones matter, in what order, and what fixing them is actually worth.

Start with the audit, not the spend

The firms that get the most from SEO are not the ones that spend the most. They are the ones that know exactly what is holding them back before they spend anything. An audit turns a vague feeling — “our marketing is not working” — into a short list of specific fixes, in the right order, with a clear sense of what each one is worth.

Run the checks above on your own firm this week. If the list that comes out is short, the firm is closer to winning than it thought. If the list is long, the good news is the same: every item on it is a fixable reason the phone is not ringing — and now you know what they are.
